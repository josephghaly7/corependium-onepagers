Depression, Eating Disorders, Anxiety

Psychiatry · Week 43 · src: Anxiety and Panic Disorder, Acute Psychosis, The Suicidal Patient
Bottom line: Suicide risk tools unreliable; anxiety may be medical; eating disorders can kill.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1For anxiety, evaluate for medical etiologies (e.g., PE) before treating psychiatrically.
↓
2For eating disorder, assess for cardiomyopathy and severe electrolyte disturbances.
↓
3For suicidal patient, search for weapons and place in cleared supervised room.
↓
4Determine need for temporary detention and emergency psychiatric evaluation.
↓
5Check all intentional ingestions: APAP, salicylate, alcohol levels, ECG, metabolic panel, pregnancy test.
↓
6For suicidal patient, consider ECG, blood (metabolic panel, VBG, toxins), urine (UA, pregnancy, drug screen), imaging.
↓
7For acutely agitated anxiety requiring rapid tranquilization, give lorazepam 1-2 mg IV/IM +/- haloperidol 5 mg IV/IM.
↓
8For severe anxiety symptoms, give lorazepam 1-2 mg PO or hydroxyzine 25-50 mg PO/IM.
↓
9For undifferentiated acute psychosis, give droperidol 5-10 mg IM or 2.5-5 mg slow IV push.
↓
10For undifferentiated acute psychosis, give haloperidol 5-10 mg slow IV push or IM, with or without benzodiazepine.

📝 Key Facts

  • No suicide risk assessment method is reliable.
  • Anxiety can be caused by medical conditions such as pulmonary embolism.
  • Eating disorders can cause cardiomyopathy and severe electrolyte disturbances.
  • Patients with eating disorders have an extraordinarily high suicide rate.
  • Anxiety diagnosis is one of exclusion because symptoms mimic life-threatening diagnoses.
  • Anxiety disorder is significantly associated with suicidal ideation and suicide attempts.
  • Indicators of heightened short-term suicide risk include a concrete plan or preparations for suicide.
  • Reduced inhibition increases suicide risk; many who attempt suicide have elevated blood alcohol levels.

Attending Pearls

  • We're taught all of these different ways to assess for suicide risk; none are actually reliable.
  • Patients with eating disorders have an extraordinarily high suicide rate.
  • Anxiety may be a pulmonary embolism in disguise.
  • Depression, anxiety, and self-harm presentations are way more common than psychosis.

Pitfalls

  • Accepting anxiety complaints at face value and treating without considering medical causes.
  • Underestimating life-threatening complications of eating disorders.
  • Relying on taught suicide risk assessment tools.
  • Simple reassurance and dismissal is an incomplete intervention.
  • A waxing and waning mental status should raise concern for delirium or underlying medical condition.
