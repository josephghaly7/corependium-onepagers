Head Trauma Etiquette

Trauma · Week 16 · src: Pediatric Head Trauma major and minor
Bottom line: Pediatric head trauma: prevent hypoxemia (SpO2 92-98%) and hypotension with age-specific SBP cutoffs.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Maintain oxygen saturation >92% and <98%; avoid prophylactic hyperventilation.
↓
2Intubate and ventilate for GCS ≤8; target PaCO2 35-45 and end-tidal CO2 30-40.
↓
3Treat hypotension with isotonic fluid boluses, blood products, and vasopressors.
↓
4For elevated ICP or impending herniation, elevate head of bed 30°.
↓
5Give 3% hypertonic saline 5 mL/kg IV over 15 min or 20% mannitol 0.25-1 g/kg IV over 20-30 min.
↓
6Maintain normal blood glucose and normothermia.
↓
7Administer seizure prophylaxis for moderate to severe TBI; treat active seizures as status epilepticus.
↓
8Obtain emergency non-contrast head CT for altered mental status, focal deficits, or penetrating trauma, with neurosurgical consult.
↓
9During first hour after severe head injury, maintain airway, adequate SBP, cervical spine protection, cranial CT, and treat herniation.

📝 Key Facts

  • Hypotension lower limits: SBP <60 mm Hg in term neonates (0-28 days), <70 mm Hg in infants (1-12 months), <70 + (2 × age) mm Hg in children 1-10 years, <90 mm Hg in children ≥10 years.
  • These hypotension thresholds define hypotension but should not be interpreted as therapeutic targets.
  • Admission SBP below the 75th percentile after severe TBI is associated with elevated in-hospital mortality.
  • Children presenting >24 hours after injury have a three-fold higher rate of TBI than those presenting within 24 hours.
  • Children <4 years old and those with hypotension, low GCS, coagulopathy, or hyperglycemia have highest morbidity and mortality.
  • Minor head injury: normal mental status, no abnormal neurologic deficits, and no physical evidence of skull fracture.
  • Oxygen saturation goal >92% and <98%; PaCO2 35-45 mm Hg and end-tidal CO2 30-40 mm Hg.
  • Abusive head trauma has higher mortality than accidental injury; ensure history matches motor development.

Attending Pearls

  • For severe head trauma, prevent secondary brain injury by avoiding hypoxemia and hypotension.
  • Treat seizures present on scene or arrival as status epilepticus, not prophylaxis.
  • Brain injury patients don't take a joke; one episode of hypotension or hypoxemia can change outcome.

Pitfalls

  • Prophylactic hyperventilation is not recommended.
  • Interpreting hypotension thresholds as therapeutic targets rather than lower limits.
  • Missing abusive head trauma by not ensuring history matches motor development.
