066

Pediatric Blood Cultures

Pediatrics · Week 25 · src: CorePendium Pediatric Fever and Serious Bacterial Infections, Pediatric Soft Tissue Infections
Bottom line: Avoid unnecessary blood cultures; PCV13/20 and Hib make occult bacteremia rare in well-appearing febrile children 3-36 months.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1If well-appearing fully vaccinated child 3-36 months with fever alone, do not obtain blood culture.
↓
2Identify organism: Staph aureus, group A/B strep, pneumococcus, Listeria, Clostridium, Candida, Cryptococcus, E. coli, Pseudomonas, H flu, gonorrhea, meningococcus are usually pathogens.
↓
3Coagulase-negative staph (any species other than aureus), Cutibacterium, Bacillus, diphtheroids are usually contaminants.
↓
4Check for intravascular device: central line, pacemaker, defibrillator, or aortic graft; if present, contaminant may be real pathogen.
↓
5Assess clinically: fever, white count, signs/symptoms of infection, and alternative explanation (e.g., viral URI).
↓
6If contaminant bug, healthy patient, no device, and doing well, no repeat culture or antibiotics needed.
↓
7If PCR cannot distinguish gram-positive cocci in clusters and patient is sick, bring back for evaluation.
↓
8If Staph aureus grows, bring patient back for admission, workup, and ID consultation.
↓
9If patient on oral antibiotic and clinically improving, continue oral therapy; no repeat culture needed.
↓
10If positive blood culture for Staph aureus, admit for workup; oral options often adequate for other pathogens.

📝 Key Facts

  • Blood culture positive in <12 hours suggests pathogen; positive at 72 hours suggests contaminant.
  • PCV13/20 and Hib vaccination make occult bacteremia rare in well-appearing children 3-36 months with fever alone.
  • Staph aureus, group A/B strep, pneumococcus, Listeria, Clostridium, Candida, Cryptococcus, E. coli, Pseudomonas, H flu, gonorrhea, meningococcus are almost always pathogens.
  • Coagulase-negative staph, Cutibacterium, Bacillus, Corynebacterium/diphtheroids are usually contaminants.
  • Intravascular devices such as pacemakers, defibrillators, central lines, and aortic grafts can make contaminant organisms clinically significant.
  • Staph aureus bacteremia can disseminate to spine, organs, and psoas muscle; ID consult improves outcomes.
  • About one-third of older females have asymptomatic bacteriuria; only about 10% of confused elderly with positive urine culture have UTI as cause.
  • Oral phosphomycin for systemic infection is about 70% bactericidal; nausea/vomiting/diarrhea limit achieving ideal dose.
  • PCR on blood culture for gram-positive cocci in clusters distinguishes coagulase-negative staph from Staph aureus.

Attending Pearls

  • Don't get a blood culture if you don't need it.
  • Blood goes there, so can staph.
  • Staph aureus is our worst bacterial enemy by far.
  • UTIs are not a lab diagnosis; UTIs are a clinical diagnosis.
  • There's nothing magical about IV antibiotics.

Pitfalls

  • Obtaining blood cultures in well-appearing fully vaccinated children over 3 months with fever alone.
  • Treating coagulase-negative staph as true bacteremia without checking for intravascular devices or clinical context.
  • Attributing altered mental status in elderly to a positive urine culture without seeking other causes.
  • Sending throat cultures in patients with prominent viral symptoms, increasing colonization confusion.
  • Assuming a positive culture in a healthy patient with an intravascular device is a contaminant; device presence warrants evaluation.
