Pediatric Mild TBI

Trauma · Week 16 · src: CorePendium Traumatic Brain Injury (Major), Traumatic Brain Injury (Minor)
Bottom line: Pediatric mild TBI (GCS 13-15): use PECARN, no routine imaging, 70-80% recover by 1-3 months.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1For pediatric mild TBI (GCS 13-15), use PECARN rule to decide if head CT is needed.
↓
2If CT considered, discuss radiation risks with family before imaging.
↓
3Do not routinely use MRI, SPECT, or skull radiographs; if worried, go straight to CT.
↓
4Use validated symptom rating scale (e.g. graded symptom checklist >6 years), not SAC alone; consider PCP referral for cognitive testing.
↓
5Do not use serum biomarkers (S100B, tau, UCH-L1, GFAP) outside research.
↓
6Discharge: advise few days rest, then gradually resume non-exercise activities while watching for symptom recurrence.
↓
7Once regular activities tolerated without symptoms, start non-contact aerobic exercise program.
↓
8For school return, gradually increase time and intensity; provide note to teacher and involve primary care.
↓
9If severe/progressive headache after mild TBI, obtain head CT; delayed injury risk outweighs radiation.
↓
10Treat headache with non-opioid analgesia (acetaminophen/ibuprofen); caution overuse causes rebound headaches.

📝 Key Facts

  • CDC 2018 pediatric mild TBI guideline includes GCS 13-15; terms concussion, minor head injury, and mild TBI used interchangeably.
  • Mild TBI = GCS 13-15, moderate = 9-12, severe ≤8; GCS 13 vs 14 has significant morbidity increase.
  • Skull radiographs have only 63% sensitivity for skull fracture and miss intracranial injury without fracture.
  • 70-80% of children with mild TBI show no significant difficulties lasting >1-3 months; 20-30% may have prolonged symptoms.
  • SAC is not accurate standalone; use validated symptom rating scale, e.g., graded symptom checklist for children >6 years.
  • Serum biomarkers (S100B, tau, UCH-L1, GFAP) not recommended; UCH-L1/GFAP studied in only 23 children.
  • For severe/progressive headache after mild TBI, guidelines recommend head CT; delayed intracranial injury risk outweighs radiation.
  • Post-concussive symptoms can last 10-14 days in adults; beyond that, outpatient follow-up/referral needed.

Attending Pearls

  • CDC recommends term mild traumatic brain injury, but 'concussion' is acceptable and less frightening for parents.
  • Guidelines provide backup for difficult parent questions; available on CDC Heads Up website.
  • Good outpatient follow-up is critical because 20-30% may have prolonged symptoms.
  • Melatonin up to 10 mg can help post-concussive insomnia, though not in CDC guidelines.
  • Kids may not be small adults, but many pediatricians are.

Pitfalls

  • Relying on skull radiographs: 63% sensitivity for skull fracture and misses intracranial injury without fracture.
  • Using SAC as standalone sideline test to determine concussion.
  • Ordering serum biomarkers (S100B, tau, UCH-L1, GFAP) for mild TBI outside research.
  • Overusing acetaminophen/ibuprofen for post-concussive headache, causing rebound headaches.
