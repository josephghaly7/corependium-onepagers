Pediatric Abdominal Pain

Pediatrics · Week 24 · src: Approach to Pediatric Abdominal Disorders
Bottom line: Pediatric abdominal pain: exclude surgical emergency, resuscitate with 20 mL/kg, check glucose, examine genitals.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Include ages birth to adolescence; constipation or appendicitis can occur any age.
↓
2Red flags: bilious emesis, lethargy, severe pain/irritability, peritoneal signs, distension, sepsis, inconsistent history.
↓
3If critical: oxygen, monitor, IV/IO access (US or IO if needed).
↓
4Give 20 mL/kg isotonic crystalloid bolus for shock; reassess and repeat; hand-push via IO if hypotensive.
↓
5Check glucose in any listless/lethargic child via i-STAT; treat hypoglycemia.
↓
6Give antibiotics for intra-abdominal flora: ceftriaxone 50 mg/kg IV q12h (max 2,000 mg) plus metronidazole 10 mg/kg IV q8h (max 500 mg).
↓
7For sepsis or hospital-acquired infection, add piperacillin-tazobactam age/weight-based dose for Pseudomonas.
↓
8Provide pain control (morphine 0.05–0.1 mg/kg IV, start at 0.05 and titrate) and ondansetron for anti-emesis.
↓
9Place NGT if gastric distension; examine genitals for torsion or hernia; check skin for bruising.
↓
10Consult pediatric surgery early for ectopic pregnancy, malrotation/volvulus, perforated viscus, or intestinal obstruction.

📝 Key Facts

  • Age categories: birth to 3 months, 3 months to 3 years, 3 years to adolescence.
  • Red flags: bilious emesis, lethargy, severe pain/irritability, peritoneal signs, abdominal distension, signs of sepsis, inconsistent history, bruising in atypical locations or patterned injuries.
  • Community-acquired intra-abdominal infections: ceftriaxone 50 mg/kg IV q12h (max 2,000 mg) plus metronidazole 10 mg/kg IV q8h (max 500 mg).
  • Piperacillin-tazobactam: 2–9 months: 80 mg/kg IV q8h (max 3,000 mg piperacillin); >9 months and ≤40 kg: 100 mg/kg IV q8h (max 3,000 mg); >40 kg: 3,000 mg IV q6h.
  • After fluid resuscitation, start IV fluids at 1.5 times maintenance rate, titrate to losses.
  • For sepsis, norepinephrine or epinephrine may be considered if pressors indicated.
  • Consider early consultation: ectopic pregnancy (OB/GYN/pediatric surgery), malrotation/volvulus (pediatric surgery), perforated viscus (pediatric surgery), intestinal obstruction (pediatric surgery).
  • Extra-abdominal causes: streptococcal pharyngitis, lower lobe pneumonia, diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).

Attending Pearls

  • Check the urine.
  • Always examine the genitals.
  • Extra-abdominal problems cause significant abdominal pain: strep throat, lower lobe pneumonia, DKA.
  • Sick child resuscitation mirrors sick adult: same initial approach, they're the same species.
  • The first two seconds decide it: running around vs star-fished on the gurney.

Pitfalls

  • If constipation is found, the diagnosis is not necessarily complete; pain may be due to a more acute issue.
  • When initial testing does not yield a certain diagnosis, serial exams have a role.
  • Do not delay surgical consult while obtaining imaging.
  • Do not forget to examine the genitals in all patients.
