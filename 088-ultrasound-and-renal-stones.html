Ultrasound and Renal Stones

Renal and Urogenital · Week 40 · src: Renal Stone Disease
Bottom line: Ultrasound-first for renal colic reduces radiation without increasing adverse events or missed diagnoses.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Assess risk: young healthy first-time renal colic → ultrasound first; elderly with AAA risk factors → CT first.
↓
2If ultrasound chosen, scan kidneys for hydronephrosis, then place probe in center of belly to look for AAA.
↓
3Push probe down and wait for peristalsis to clear bowel gas if aorta not initially visible.
↓
4Add bladder view to look for stone at UVJ; apply color Doppler for twinkle artifact.
↓
5If stone, hydronephrosis, and hematuria present, diagnosis confirmed; no further imaging needed.
↓
6If only unilateral hydronephrosis with classic story and hematuria, treat as renal colic.
↓
7For repeat patient with very high pretest probability, clinically diagnose without imaging.
↓
8If patient returns with recurrent symptoms and no confirmed diagnosis, obtain advanced imaging.
↓
9Consider KUB only as courtesy for urologist follow-up; not routine.
↓
10In pregnant patients, ultrasound is first-line; reserve CT as last option; MRI second-line if ultrasound equivocal.

📝 Key Facts

  • NEJM 2015 trial: ~3,000 patients; no difference in adverse events between CT, radiology US, and POCUS arms; CT arm had more radiation over the entire length of study.
  • AUA guideline: noncontrast CT preferred initial imaging for index patient; sensitivity 98%, specificity 97%.
  • STONE score 5 factors: male sex, <6 hours of pain prior to presentation, non-Black, vomiting, and hematuria on urine dipstick.
  • Hematuria (>5 RBCs/high-power field) present in 85%-90% of confirmed urolithiasis cases; sensitivity 84%, specificity 48%, PPV 72%, NPV 65%.
  • Absence of hematuria does not rule out stone: ~33% have <5 RBCs/hpf, 19% have <1 RBC/hpf, 11% have no RBCs on UA in confirmed urolithiasis.
  • Pyuria (>10 WBCs/hpf) present in ~14.2% of renal colic patients; obtain urine cultures in those with pyuria or positive leukocyte esterase because 36.4% will have positive cultures.
  • CT identifies alternative diagnoses in ~10.8% of patients undergoing CT for renal colic; ~11%-15% of urolithiasis patients lack hydronephrosis on US; if hydronephrosis present, obstructing stone likely >5 mm.
  • POCUS performed by emergency physicians: sensitivity 70%-85%, specificity 50%-92%; radiology US: sensitivity up to 98.3%, specificity up to 100%.
  • STONE score not validated in certain races and does not predict clinical outcomes including need for urologic intervention.
  • C-reactive protein >5 times baseline is significant predictor of UTI in renal colic.

Attending Pearls

  • Ultrasound-first approach reduces radiation without increasing adverse events or missing critical diagnoses; fewer cumulative CTs in 3-6 months after ED visit.
  • If concerned about AAA, don't rely on ultrasound — go straight to CT; almost every experienced EP has missed a AAA thinking it was a kidney stone.
  • Proximity to the CT scanner is not an indication for the study.
  • If you see hydro, they have a good story and blood in urine, you're done — unless another reason to suspect something else.
  • Diagnostic closure is for the provider; consider giving urine strainer and letting patient diagnose themselves in 3 days when they pee it out.

Pitfalls

  • CT scanning everyone due to convenience, CYA, or proximity to scanner; proximity is not an indication for the study.
  • Ordering CT solely to know stone size; many large stones pass without surgery — up to 50% of stones >9 mm pass on their own.
  • Giving up on aorta ultrasound due to bowel gas; push harder with probe and wait for peristalsis/air to clear.
  • Routine KUB for stone follow-up; only order if urologist requests it.
  • Relying on ultrasound in elderly/first-time renal colic patients with AAA risk factors; go straight to CT.
