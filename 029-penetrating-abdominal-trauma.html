Penetrating Abdominal Trauma

Trauma · Week 14 · src: Penetrating Abdominal Trauma, Blunt Abdominal Trauma
Bottom line: Penetrating abdominal trauma: know the 5 OR indications, evaluate peritoneal violation, observe selectively.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Assess for immediate OR indications: hemodynamic instability, peritonitis, evisceration, gross blood in emesis/rectum, implement in situ.
↓
2If any OR indication present, call trauma/acute care surgery and proceed directly to operating room—minutes affect mortality.
↓
3If intubation needed, use ketamine 1-2 mg/kg IV push over 1 minute (0.5-1 mg/kg in shock); address tension pneumothorax or tamponade before intubation.
↓
4If hemorrhagic shock: activate massive transfusion protocol, minimize crystalloid, allow permissive hypotension (unless significant head injury), perform eFAST.
↓
5Treat pain: fentanyl 0.25-1 µg/kg IV push (0.75-1 µg/kg for severe); subdissociative ketamine 0.1-0.3 mg/kg IV infusion over 15-30 minutes.
↓
6In stable patients, diagnostic evaluation has two steps: identify peritoneal violation (step 1), then intraabdominal injury (step 2).
↓
7Local wound exploration: sterile surgical procedure looking for intact anterior rectus fascia; if fascia intact, wash out wound and discharge with follow-up.
↓
8If peritoneal violation or intraabdominal injury suspected: CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast for stable GSW; serial exams and/or CT for stab wounds.
↓
9If no intraabdominal injury but peritoneal violation present, admit for observation with serial abdominal exams for 12-24 hours; DPL in austere environments.
↓
10During observation monitor for peritonitis, leukocytosis, or hemodynamic instability; if develop, take to OR.

📝 Key Facts

  • Big five OR indications: hemodynamic instability, peritonitis, evisceration, gross blood in emesis or on rectal exam, implement in situ (weapon still in place).
  • About half of wounds that penetrate the peritoneum have a significant intraabdominal injury.
  • About one-third of thoracoabdominal wounds have a diaphragmatic injury; CT is not sensitive for these.
  • CT can miss hollow viscus and diaphragmatic injuries; a 2013 USC study showed serial clinical exams are more sensitive than CT for selecting patients needing therapeutic laparotomy.
  • Local wound exploration: if anterior rectus fascia is intact, peritoneum not violated—wash out wound and discharge with follow-up.
  • Diagnostic evaluation answers two separate questions: peritoneal violation (step 1) and intraabdominal injury (step 2).
  • A patient with peritoneal violation but no identified intraabdominal injury does not require surgery—can be observed (institution dependent).
  • Diaphragm excursion: fourth rib anteriorly to seventh rib posteriorly down to costal margin with respiration; anything below nipple line is thoracoabdominal.

Attending Pearls

  • Minutes really do affect mortality in this population—know the OR indications and get on that trajectory as soon as possible.
  • Diagnostic evaluation in penetrating abdominal trauma answers two separate questions: peritoneal violation and intraabdominal injury.
  • Your CT is not always the tube of truth when it comes to penetrating abdominal trauma.
  • Better to be stabbed in the belly than shot in the belly (in the era of selective nonoperative management).
  • Local wound exploration is a legit procedure: sterile technique, good lighting, well anesthetized, may need procedural sedation.

Pitfalls

  • Using blunt probing of the wound with a Q-tip to assess peritoneal violation; it is unreliable and cannot rule out violation.
  • Relying on a negative CT to discharge a patient with possible peritoneal violation—CT may miss hollow viscus or diaphragmatic injuries.
  • Forgetting the thoracoabdominal region: wounds below the nipple line can involve diaphragm, pericardium, or thorax.
  • Assuming gunshot wound trajectory based on external wound location—bullets ricochet off bones and trajectory is variable.
  • Not ensuring observation occurs at an institution with a robust protocol for selective nonoperative management and serial exams.
