Painful Red Eye

Ophthalmology · Week 35 · src: Uveitis, Preseptal and Orbital Cellulitis, Endophthalmitis
Bottom line: Red painful eye: get acuity, front-to-back exam, steroids only after ophthalmology; refer uveitis within 24h.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Obtain visual acuity; it is the vital sign of the eye.
↓
2Step back and assess the big picture before focusing on the eyeball.
↓
3Examine front to back: eyelids and lacrimal area first.

📝 Key Facts

  • Uveitis is an ocular emergency; treatment should be initiated promptly to improve visual outcomes.
  • Uveitis anatomy: anterior = iritis (iris) or iridocyclitis (iris and ciliary body); intermediate = cyclitis (ciliary body); posterior = choroiditis (choroid and iris) or chorioretinitis (choroid and retina); panuveitis = all structures.
  • Anterior uveitis typically presents with pain, red eye, photophobia; posterior uveitis typically presents with floaters and blurry vision or loss of vision.
  • Slit lamp finding of inflammatory cells in the anterior chamber (turbid aqueous humor), termed cells and flare, is diagnostic.
  • Among the sight-threatening causes, endophthalmitis is infection of both aqueous and vitreous; hypopyon should raise high suspicion and trigger immediate ophthalmology consult.
  • Limbus is where the cornea meets the sclera; keratitis is inflammation of the cornea.
  • Visual acuity at presentation is the primary predictor of overall prognosis and visual recovery in endophthalmitis.
  • Preseptal cellulitis is more common than orbital cellulitis (83% vs 17% of cases in one study).

Attending Pearls

  • Don't lose the forest for the trees; stand back and see the big picture.
  • Steroids can temporarily improve symptoms but are dangerous in infections like herpes.
  • Visual acuity at presentation is the primary predictor of overall prognosis and visual recovery.
  • All patients with uveitis should be referred for ophthalmology follow-up within 24 hours for a dilated exam and further evaluation for an underlying etiology.
  • The clinical features that distinguish orbital from preseptal cellulitis: pain with extraocular movements, ophthalmoplegia, visual disturbance, proptosis.

Pitfalls

  • Failing to obtain visual acuity early, especially when vision is abnormal.
  • Jumping straight to the eyeball without checking eyelids, lashes, and lacrimal area.
  • Using steroids for an inflamed eye without ophthalmology follow-up, especially with herpetic infection.
  • In uveitis, starting corticosteroids before consulting ophthalmology.
  • Failure to promptly diagnose and adequately treat orbital cellulitis, risking endophthalmitis, cavernous sinus thrombosis, meningitis, empyema, brain abscess, visual loss.
