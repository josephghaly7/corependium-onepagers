Generalized Weakness

Neurology · Week 17 · src: Approach to Generalized Weakness
Bottom line: Generalized weakness: stabilize ABCs, rule out focal patterns, check glucose, exposure.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Place on cardiac monitor, continuous pulse oximetry; secure IV access; obtain point-of-care glucose.
↓
2Have advanced airway equipment at bedside; consider reversible causes (hypoglycemia, opioid toxicity) before intubation.
↓
3If glucose <55 mg/dL, give dextrose 50% in water 50 mL [25 g] IV once or dextrose 10% in water 250 mL (25 g) IV rapid infusion.
↓
4Intubate for increasing weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, dyspnea, rapid shallow breathing, weak cough, interrupted speech, accessory muscles, paradoxical breathing, inability to lift head, decreased LOC, NIF 0 to -30 cm water, vital capacity <15-20 mL/kg, hypoxemia/hypercapnia.
↓
5Use non-depolarizing paralytic (eg, rocuronium) for RSI in undifferentiated patients or if hyperkalemia likely (renal failure, heat stroke, chronic neuromuscular disease).
↓
6Provide oxygen by nasal cannula, non-rebreather, or NIPPV as needed; maintain oxygen saturation >90%.
↓
7Give IV fluids for hypotension/hypoperfusion; obtain ECG.
↓
8Check focal neurologic findings (Glasgow Coma Scale, pupils, deep tendon reflexes); do not give dextrose unless glucose <55 mg/dL.
↓
9Expose patient fully: assess trauma, drug use, pain medication patches, infected wounds; check core temperature.
↓
10If no focal pattern, pursue systems workup: point-of-care glucose, ECG, troponin, CBC, chemistry, thyroid tests, urinalysis; admission if not back to baseline or etiology unclear.

⏱ Time Targets

Symptom onset acuteseconds to minuteseg, STEMI, dysrhythmia, stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, hypoglycemia, poisoning, cauda equina syndrome
Symptom onset subacutehours to daysSepsis, cauda equina, infection, medications, dehydration, electrolyte derangements, myasthenia gravis, botulism, Guillain-Barré syndrome
Symptom onset chronicweeks to monthsALS, anemia, physical fatigue, rheumatologic disorders, psychiatric disorders
30-day serious condition risk in elderly nonspecific complaintsnearly 60%Nemec et al. reviewed 218 elderly patients; serious condition defined as potentially life-threatening or requiring early intervention within 30 days

Dextrose 50% in water50 mL [25 g] IV onceif glucose <55 mg/dL [<3.1 mmol/L]
Dextrose 10% in water250 mL (25 g) IV rapid infusionif glucose <55 mg/dL [<3.1 mmol/L]
Rocuroniumnon-depolarizing paralytic for RSIrecommended for undifferentiated patients or if hyperkalemia likely (renal failure, heat stroke, chronic neuromuscular disease)
Succinylcholine3 mg/kg (doubled dose)in myasthenic crisis due to decreased postsynaptic acetylcholine receptors
Non-depolarizing agents (eg, rocuronium, vecuronium)decrease dose by halfin myasthenic crisis due to increased potency

📝 Key Facts

  • Weakness affects up to 10% of ED patients.
  • Generalized weakness 65% vs focal weakness 35%; generalized causes infectious 32%, metabolic 18%, somatic 10%, malignant 10%; localized weakness intracranial cause 76%.
  • Guillain-Barré syndrome is the most common cause of non-traumatic, non-stroke acute flaccid paralysis in Western countries.
  • Patients with vague symptoms such as generalized weakness have increased 7-day mortality; incorrect ED diagnoses up to 50%.
  • Nickel et al.: ~76% of patients with weakness had final diagnosis of TIA, cerebral ischemia, or intracerebral hemorrhage.
  • Hyponatremia led to weakness in up to 22% of patients; anemia made up ~36.4% of hospitalizations in nonspecific complaints.
  • In myasthenic crisis, up to 90% require intubation; succinylcholine less potent (double dose to 3 mg/kg), non-depolarizing agents increased potency (decrease dose by half).
  • Glucose <55 mg/dL [<3.1 mmol/L] is threshold for dextrose; maintain oxygen saturation >90%; NIF 0 to -30 cm water and vital capacity <15-20 mL/kg favor intubation.

Attending Pearls

  • Generalized weakness is that chief complaint that pops up and it's a giant game of chicken on who's not going to take it.
  • Distinguishing focal weakness from generalized weakness is an important first step.
  • Weakness patterns guide differential: lateralizing=stroke; ascending=GBS/tick; both legs=cord compression; bulbar=MG/Miller Fisher/botulism; upper>lower=central cord.
  • Expose the patient head to toe; there are mysteries and you are there to solve them.
  • In undifferentiated critical weakness, err on the side of safety with RSI medications.

Pitfalls

  • Intubating before checking glucose or considering opioid toxicity removes the assessment.
  • Failing to fully expose and roll the patient can miss trauma, infection, or patches.
  • Assuming a code stroke without checking for non-focal weakness patterns can misdiagnose.
  • Missing focal weakness pattern can send workup down wrong pathway.
  • Using succinylcholine in progressive neuromuscular diseases or prolonged immobilization (Guillain-Barré, multiple sclerosis, ALS) risks hyperkalemia and lethal dysrhythmia.
