Urology Suite: Stone Cold Facts

Renal and Urogenital · Week 40 · 20 min
Bottom line: Assess clinical status, stone size, infection risk; use NSAIDs, selective imaging.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Assess clinical status: febrile, tachycardic, hypotensive, or failing eyeball test.
↓
2Assess pain level and whether pain can be managed outpatient after prior meds.
↓
3Review CT for stone size, location, hydronephrosis, and renal anatomy.
↓
4If stone <5 mm, pain controlled, not sick: trial of passage with outpatient follow-up.
↓
5If stone 5-7 mm: 50-60% passage chance; consider urology follow-up or intervention.
↓
6If stone >7 mm: <30% passage chance; likely needs surgical intervention.
↓
7If obstructing stone but stable and pain controlled: discharge with close follow-up and return precautions.
↓
8If suspected infection/sepsis: use clinical status, not UA alone; admit if sick or obstructing.
↓
9If gray area: consider comorbidities, social situation, reliability; call urologist if concerned.
↓
10If discharging with suspected infection: prescribe oral cephalosporin or Bactrim and close follow-up.

📝 Key Facts

  • Stones <5 mm pass spontaneously >90% of the time within 2-4 weeks.
  • Stones 5-7 mm pass in 50-60%; >7 mm pass in <30%.
  • Stone size does not correlate with pain; small stones may need intervention, large stones may be painless.
  • In healthy two-kidney patients, unilateral obstruction may not raise creatinine; other kidney can increase function up to 185%.
  • Common stone impaction sites: UPJ, over iliac vessels, and UVJ.
  • Staghorn calculi are infection nidus; sepsis more often occurs during PCNL treatment, not initial ER presentation.

Attending Pearls

  • Use the eyeball test; septic stone patients look miserable, diaphoretic, and fatigued.
  • Ketorolac/NSAIDs help stone pain much more than opiates; educate patients.
  • UA is not end-all; clinical status and gestalt drive disposition.
  • Never hesitate to call your local urologist; they welcome consults and can arrange follow-up.

Pitfalls

  • Jumping straight to opiates; NSAIDs/ketorolac work better for stone pain.
  • Over-relying on UA; WBC/RBC expected with stones, clinical status matters more.
  • Sending home obstructing infected stones; that is sepsis waiting to happen.
  • Assuming elevated creatinine requires intervention; not absolute unless solitary kidney or bilateral obstruction.
