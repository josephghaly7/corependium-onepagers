History of Opioid Epidemic

Toxicology · Week 38 · src: Opioid Toxicity and Poisoning, Opioid Use Disorder and Dependence
Bottom line: Opioid epidemic from misleading research and marketing: for every overdose death, 825 non-medical users.

📝 Key Facts

  • Overdose deaths exceed US deaths from AIDS and murder combined and surpass car crash deaths.
  • Porter and Jick 1980 letter: reviewed over 11,000 inpatients receiving at least one narcotic; only 4 cases of well-documented addiction and 1 considered major.
  • OxyContin released 1996; promoted as lasting 12 hours, but evidence shows it lasted about 6 hours in most patients.
  • In the decade after pain became the fifth vital sign (policy instituted in 2001), opioid overdose deaths quadrupled.
  • Prescriptions for opioid analgesics increased 7-fold between 1997 and 2007.
  • 2011 was the first peak in opioid prescriptions before dipping.
  • Four out of five heroin users started on prescription pills.
  • In 2013, fentanyl overdose deaths were less than 300 per month; by 2015, approximately 1,000 per month.
  • Fentanyl is worth over 30 times its weight in gold.
  • Approximately 30,000 Americans die yearly due to opiates, about half from injectable forms and half from pills.
  • For every one death, there are 10 treatment admissions, 32 emergency department visits, 130 individuals who abuse or are dependent, and 825 non-medical users.

Attending Pearls

  • The five Ps of disaster: Porter/Jick, pharma promotion, pain as fifth vital sign, purging pill mills, proliferation of heroin and fentanyl.
  • Fentanyl is extremely cheap to produce and potent; a tiny amount can cut heroin and boost perceived potency.
  • Most heroin users became addicted through prescription opioids, not direct transition to street drugs.
  • For every overdose death, there are 825 non-medical users—lives ruined extend far beyond deaths.

Pitfalls

  • Using Porter and Jick to justify chronic opioid use; it was a brief letter with no rigorous analysis, just inpatient data.
  • Believing OxyContin's 12-hour duration and lower addiction risk; evidence shows ~6-hour duration and higher addiction risk.
  • Asking a pain score without a plan to treat it; it creates pressure to prescribe opioids.
  • Assuming abuse-deterrent formulations prevent abuse; users find ways around them.
