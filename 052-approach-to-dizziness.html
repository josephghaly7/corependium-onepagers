Approach to Dizziness

Neurology · Week 19 · 27 min
Bottom line: Dizziness: assess sick vs not, avoid anchoring, focus on timing/triggers/exam.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Assess sick or not sick; if ill-appearing, start ABCs, IV/O2, monitor, fingerstick glucose, EKG, and pads.
↓
2If not ill-appearing, proceed with thorough history and physical rather than immediate testing.
↓
3Ask patient to describe dizziness in own words; do not force into vertigo, presyncope, or disequilibrium.
↓
4Clarify timing, triggers, duration, and context of dizziness.
↓
5Ask about associated neurologic symptoms, vision changes, hearing changes, weakness, gait.
↓
6If reproductive-age female, obtain point-of-care HCG; consider hemoglobin, glucose, EKG in older patients.
↓
7If PE suspected, consider D-dimer; if vague complaint or risk factors, broaden workup and observe.
↓
8Perform neuro, cardiac, pulmonary, ENT exam; assess gait, truncal ataxia, strength, sensation, visual fields.
↓
9If true vertical nystagmus is present, obtain imaging for possible central stroke.
↓
10If vesicles in external auditory canal, consider Ramsey Hunt syndrome and treat accordingly.

📝 Key Facts

  • Dizziness is a top 10 chief complaint for ED and outpatient visits, accounting for millions of US visits yearly.
  • Patients may describe dizziness differently when asked again 15 minutes later; classification reliability is poor.
  • More than 50% of dizziness is non-neurologic; consider cardiac, pulmonary, GI bleed, sepsis, endocrine.
  • True vertical nystagmus is rare and highly correlated with central stroke, requiring imaging.
  • Vesicles in external auditory canal suggest Ramsey Hunt syndrome with vestibular symptoms.
  • Consider EKG in older patients with first-time dizziness; hemoglobin may reveal anemia as cause.

Attending Pearls

  • Dizziness workup is complex; practice varies widely, so keep an open mind and reassess.
  • Timing, triggers, associated symptoms, and context are as important as the traditional classification.
  • Great history takers paint a picture of the patient before and after the event.
  • Sick dizzy patients often have non-neurologic emergencies; ABCs and EKG come first.

Pitfalls

  • Anchoring on an initial dizziness category can miss stroke, especially if patient changes description.
  • Do not pigeonhole patients into vertigo, presyncope, or disequilibrium too quickly.
  • Failing to examine eyes for vertical nystagmus or get patient walking can miss central causes.
  • Skipping EKG in older dizzy patients may miss cardiac cause.
