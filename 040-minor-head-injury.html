Minor Head Injury

Trauma · Week 16 · src: Traumatic Brain Injury Minor, Pediatric Head Trauma major and minor
Bottom line: Minor head injury = GCS 15; use CT decision rules, observe 6 hours, caution with anticoagulants.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Assess GCS and neuro exam; minor head injury = GCS 15 after nontrivial head blow.
↓
2If GCS <15 at 2 hours or focal neuro deficit, obtain head CT.
↓
3Nexus II: skull fracture, basilar signs, scalp hematoma, neuro deficit, altered consciousness, coagulopathy, persistent vomiting, age >65 → CT.
↓
4Canadian Head CT high risk: GCS <15 at 2 hours, open/depressed skull fracture, basilar skull fracture signs → CT.
↓
5Canadian medium risk: retrograde amnesia ≥30 minutes or dangerous mechanism → CT recommended.
↓
6New Orleans criteria (GCS 15, no deficits): headache, vomiting, age >60, intoxication, anterograde amnesia, seizure, visible trauma above clavicle → CT.
↓
7If no CT scanner and low risk, observe 6 hours; if no progressive headache/vomiting, discharge with return precautions.
↓
8If anticoagulated/antiplatelet, lower threshold and obtain immediate head CT.
↓
9If initial CT negative in anticoagulated patient, observe with neuro checks every 2–4 hours; repeat CT if deterioration.
↓
10For high-risk elderly or multi-anticoagulated patients with negative CT, consider repeat CT; East guidelines admit if INR supratherapeutic.
↓
11For elevated ICP in pediatric severe TBI: elevate head 30°, give hypertonic saline or mannitol, maintain normothermia/normal glucose.
↓
12If pediatric seizures present, treat as status epilepticus, not prophylaxis.

📝 Key Facts

  • Minor head injury: GCS 15, awake/oriented, possibly brief LOC; GCS 13–14 at 30 minutes is moderate.
  • All six CT decision tools are 97–99% sensitive but poorly specific.
  • Nexus II criteria: skull fracture, basilar skull fracture, scalp hematoma, neurologic deficit, altered consciousness, coagulopathy, persistent vomiting, age >65.
  • Canadian Head CT exclusions: age <16, blood thinners, seizure after injury; high risk includes GCS <15 at 2 hours and suspected open/depressed skull fracture.
  • New Orleans criteria: headache, vomiting, age >60, alcohol/drug intoxication, persistent anterograde amnesia, seizure, visible trauma above clavicle.
  • Anticoagulated patients with negative initial CT have 2.5% risk of delayed bleed; Plavix is as bad as Coumadin for delayed intracranial bleeding.
  • Pediatric hypotension thresholds: SBP <60 (term neonate 0–28 days), <70 (infant 1–12 months), <70 + (2×age) (1–10 years), <90 (≥10 years).
  • Abusive head trauma has higher mortality than accidental injury; history must match motor development.

Attending Pearls

  • Clinical signs of basilar skull fracture are more sensitive in aggregate than CT; CT is for associated intracranial hemorrhage.
  • Six-hour observation with no progressive headache or vomiting makes acute deterioration very unlikely.
  • Risk stratify anticoagulated patients: young on low-level anticoagulation with negative CT can go home; elderly on Coumadin/Plavix/aspirin warrant repeat CT.
  • Concussion symptoms are global (memory, concentration, nausea, irritability); focal symptoms require re-imaging.
  • Pediatric TBI: primary goal is preventing secondary injury by avoiding hypoxemia and hypotension; oxygen saturation >92% and <98%, CO2 35–45 mm Hg, EtCO2 30–40 mm Hg.

Pitfalls

  • Calling GCS 13–14 minor head injury; it should be considered moderate.
  • Using decision rules to justify more CTs, increasing radiation without improving outcomes.
  • Sending anticoagulated elderly patients home without considering delayed subdural and repeat imaging.
  • Attributing focal neurologic findings to concussion; they suggest structural injury or delayed bleed.
  • Performing prophylactic hyperventilation in pediatric TBI; avoid it.
