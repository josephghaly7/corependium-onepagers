Tubo-ovarian Abscess

OB/GYN · Week 41 · src: Pelvic Inflammatory Disease
Bottom line: TOA is a polymicrobial pelvic abscess; suspect with PID symptoms, fever, or failed PID therapy.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Suspect TOA in premenopausal women 15-40 with lower abdominal pain, fever, chills, discharge, or failed PID treatment.
↓
2Perform speculum and bimanual exam; look for mucopurulent discharge, cervical motion tenderness, adnexal mass.
↓
3Obtain pregnancy test, basic labs, STI labs; add blood cultures if sick appearing; ESR/CRP may indicate severity.
↓
4If ill-appearing, adnexal mass, significant pain, or failed antibiotics, order imaging.
↓
5Use transvaginal ultrasound (sensitivity 75-90%) or CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast (sensitivity 78-100%).
↓
6If pregnant, prefer ultrasound or MRI if available; if nondiagnostic or unavailable, use CT.
↓
7Start empiric IV ceftriaxone plus doxycycline plus metronidazole; if toxic or septic, use broad-spectrum antibiotics.
↓
8Consult OB-GYN early because antibiotics fail in up to 30% of cases.
↓
9Consider operative intervention if abscess >5-7 cm, rupture, peritonitis, older age, or high failure risk.
↓
10Once improved, transition IV to oral antibiotics for at least 14 days.

📝 Key Facts

  • TOA most common in women aged 15-40; about one-third of admitted PID patients have TOA.
  • Rupture occurs in about 15% of cases; about 20% meet sepsis criteria; mortality around 4% if either occurs.
  • Up to 90% present with lower abdominal pain; fever in 50-90%; chills in about 50%; nausea in about 25%.
  • Transvaginal ultrasound sensitivity is 75-90%; CT sensitivity is 78-100%, closer to 90-95% with newer CTs.
  • Antibiotics fail in up to 30% of TOA cases; abscesses over 5-7 cm have higher failure risk.
  • MRI sensitivity for TOA is about 95% and specificity about 80%, but often unavailable in EDs.
  • Risk of PID with IUD insertion is very low, but most risk is in the first three weeks.
  • ESR and CRP may be associated with more severe disease but are nonspecific.

Attending Pearls

  • Think TOA if patient is toxic appearing, has severe pelvic pain, adnexal mass, or failed PID therapy.
  • Fever is much less common in PID, so think TOA if the patient is febrile.
  • CT is the go-to when concerned about other organs, older age, malignancy risk, or rupture.
  • With confirmed TOA and IUD, OB-GYN usually removes the IUD, balancing pregnancy risk after recent intercourse.
  • Tubo-ovarian actinomycosis forms multiple abscesses with solid-appearing enhancing areas but responds to standard antibiotics.

Pitfalls

  • Do not rely on history and exam alone; they are nonspecific and not definitive for TOA.
  • Do not skip speculum and bimanual exam; they are needed despite poor sensitivity.
  • Do not use transabdominal ultrasound; transvaginal ultrasound is much more accurate.
  • Do not cover only STIs; include polymicrobial GI bacteria like E. coli, Bacteroides, and anaerobes.
  • Do not forget OB-GYN consultation; antibiotics fail in up to 30% and some need operative intervention.
