Pediatric Fever: Neonates and Infants

Pediatrics · Week 25 · src: Pediatric Fever and Serious Bacterial Infections
Bottom line: Febrile infants ≤28 days need full workup, IV antibiotics and admission; >38°C is fever.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Assess critical patient: ABCs, IV, O2, monitor, check glucose.
↓
2If hypoglycemic neonate, give D10 5 mL/kg IV (rule of 50s).
↓
3Start fluid resuscitation with 10 mL/kg NS bolus in neonates; reassess frequently.
↓
4For older infants (e.g., 90-day-old), consider 20 mL/kg NS bolus.
↓
5If circulation inadequate, start epinephrine or norepinephrine.
↓
6Give empiric antibiotics/antivirals based on suspected etiology.
↓
7Consider high-flow warmed humidified nasal cannula for non-invasive support; intubate if needed.
↓
8Rectal temperature is most accurate; fever defined as >38°C (100.4°F).
↓
9Neonates ≤28 days: full workup, IV antibiotics, and admission.
↓
10Infants 29-60 days: management depends on inflammatory markers and UA.

📝 Key Facts

  • Fever in an infant is defined as >38°C or 100.4°F.
  • Neonates and young infants (≤60 days) are evaluated and treated differently; obtain inflammatory markers.
  • Positive inflammatory markers: temperature >38.5°C, procalcitonin >0.5 ng/mL, CRP >20 mg/L, ANC >4000 mm³.
  • Most fevers in children are viral.
  • In patients 0-28 days, full workup, IV antibiotics and admission is generally appropriate (some variation allowed in 22-28 day group).
  • All ill-appearing and immunocompromised patients should be evaluated for serious bacterial infections regardless of age.
  • Early antibiotics should be prioritized before LP if bacterial infection (especially meningitis or sepsis) is highly suspected.
  • Rates of bacteremia are highest in newborns and decrease stepwise with each week of life.

Attending Pearls

  • Pediatric sepsis does not require hypotension for diagnosis and thus may be under-recognized.
  • Resuscitation of a sick neonate follows the same ABC approach as adults, just with different sizes and glucose concentrations.
  • Do not memorize all branch points; look up algorithms as needed and focus on the big picture.
  • The AAP guidelines focus on well-appearing infants; always address the critical patient first.
  • You can only apply clinical prediction tools under the right circumstances.

Pitfalls

  • Neonates and young infants (≤60 days) are evaluated and treated differently than older, vaccinated, clinically evaluable children.
  • Do not rely on BP to diagnose pediatric sepsis; hypotension is not required.
  • Do not forget to check glucose in altered mental status or seizure with fever.
  • Do not delay antibiotics for LP if bacterial infection is highly suspected.
  • Do not apply clinical prediction tools without checking inclusion and exclusion criteria.
