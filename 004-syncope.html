Syncope

Cardiology · Week 2 · src: Approach to Syncope, ECG Diagnosis Syncope
Bottom line: Syncope equals near syncope; negative ED workup usually allows discharge.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Perform thorough history and physical; ask about prodrome, chest pain, SOB, headache, back/abdominal pain, palpitations.
↓
2Obtain ECG in all; look for ischemia, arrhythmia, long QT, short PR, Brugada, HCM, ARVC, ASD.
↓
3Place all patients on cardiac monitoring for 2-3 hours in the ED.
↓
4If syncope plus CHF or cirrhosis, check electrolytes and hematocrit; otherwise labs guided by history.
↓
5Do not check troponin for pure syncope without ACS symptoms; ACS rarely presents with syncope alone.
↓
6If ECG shows long QT, Brugada, or WPW after syncope, admit for monitoring and cardiology/EP.
↓
7If long QT has reversible cause (hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia, medications), correct it in ED.
↓
8If ECG, labs, monitoring normal and patient back to baseline, discharge with close follow-up.
↓
9Admit if history suggests ACS or arrhythmia: chest pain, palpitations, no prodrome, exertional/rest syncope.
↓
10Use Canadian Syncope Risk Score for intermediate-risk patients; 0 points supports discharge, 1+ supports admission/workup.

📝 Key Facts

  • 10 seconds of interrupted blood flow to both cerebral cortices or brainstem RAS causes loss of consciousness.
  • Syncope accounts for 1.2% of all ED visits (~1.7 million annually in US).
  • Common causes: vasovagal 21%, orthostatic 9%, medication-related 7%, unknown 37%.
  • Serious etiology in ~10% of ED syncope presentations; cardiac dysrhythmias account for 2-3%.
  • Near syncope and true syncope share same etiologies, workup, and prognosis; treat them identically.
  • A 72-hour admission diagnoses syncope in about 50%; 80% of those are diagnosed during initial ED hours.
  • Canadian Syncope Risk Score: 0 points = 1.9% 30-day risk; 1+ points = medium risk starting at 3%.
  • Absence of prodrome before syncope is the best predictor of arrhythmic cause.

Attending Pearls

  • Spend extra time on history and physical; ask about prodrome, post-event symptoms, back pain, headache.
  • If ED workup is negative, hospitalization rarely changes outcome; arrange close outpatient follow-up.
  • Use Canadian Syncope Risk Score for middle-zone patients and document it to support disposition.
  • Sudden loss at rest or exertional syncope is concerning, especially in older adults, even with normal ED evaluation.

Pitfalls

  • Assuming near syncope is less dangerous than syncope; outcomes are the same.
  • Ordering troponin for pure syncope without ACS features; ACS rarely presents with syncope alone.
  • Discharging patients with high-risk ECG findings after syncope without admission or EP consultation.
  • Admitting all syncope patients; low yield if ED workup negative and patient is back to baseline.
