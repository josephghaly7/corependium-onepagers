Pediatric Fever: Fever in Two Months to Three Years Old

Pediatrics · Week 25 · src: Pediatric Fever and Serious Bacterial Infections
Bottom line: For 3 months to 3 years, fever workup is guided by appearance; most fevers are viral.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1If ill-appearing or immunocompromised at any age, evaluate for serious bacterial infections.
↓
2If febrile neonate ≤28 days: full workup, IV antibiotics, and admission.
↓
3If 29-60 days: management depends on inflammatory markers and urinalysis.
↓
4If 60-90 days and ill-appearing: full septic workup, antibiotics, admit.
↓
5If 60-90 days and well-appearing with bronchiolitis: supportive care plus UA/UC.
↓
6If 60-90 days and well-appearing without bronchiolitis: UA/UC; consider CBC, inflammatory markers, blood culture.
↓
7If altered mental status with fever: consider CNS infection; check glucose; antibiotics and LP often indicated.
↓
8If highly suspect meningitis or sepsis: give antibiotics before LP.
↓
9For 3 months to 3 years: most fevers are viral; no routine full septic workup if well-appearing.
↓
10Treat hypoglycemia and hypocalcemia concomitantly.

Antibiotics before LPprioritize before LPWhen bacterial infection (especially meningitis or sepsis) is highly suspected.

Ceftazidime50-70 mg/kg IV every 8 hours (maximum single dose 2,000 mg)For suspected sepsis/meningitis in ≥29 days of age (Regimen 2).
Vancomycin (1 month to 8 years)20 mg/kg IV every 6 hours (maximum dose 900 mg)For suspected sepsis/meningitis in ≥29 days of age.
Vancomycin (9-16 years)17.5 mg/kg IV every 6 hours (maximum dose 900 mg)For suspected sepsis/meningitis in ≥29 days of age.
Dexamethasone0.15 mg/kg IV every 6 hours (maximum single dose 10 mg)In suspected bacterial meningitis; administer before antibiotics when possible to avoid hearing loss in Haemophilus influenzae type B meningitis.
Norepinephrine0.1-2 μg/kg/minute IV infusionWarm shock (vasodilation with strong pulses, pink extremities, brisk capillary refill); may be preferred in septic shock without cardiac dysfunction.
Epinephrine0.05-0.3 μg/kg/minute IV infusionFluid non-responsive cold shock (weak peripheral pulses, cool distal extremities, prolonged capillary refill).
Dopamine2-20 μg/kg/minute IV infusionFluid non-responsive cold shock.
Milrinone0.25-0.75 μg/kg/minute IV infusion; consider loading IV bolus 50 μg/kgFluid non-responsive cold shock.
Hydrocortisone2-3 mg/kg IV push, then 1-2 mg/kg IV push every 6 hoursCatecholamine-resistant shock or if risk exists for adrenal insufficiency.
Intravenous bolus20 mL/kg, repeatedTo achieve adequate perfusion and hemodynamic stability.

📝 Key Facts

  • Fever in an infant is defined as >38°C or 100.4°F.
  • Rectal temperature is the most accurate method of measurement.
  • Most fevers in children are viral in etiology.
  • Positive inflammatory markers include: temperature >38.5°C (101.3°F); procalcitonin >0.5 ng/mL; CRP >20 mg/L; absolute neutrophil count >4000 mm³.
  • Neonates and young infants (≤60 days) are evaluated and treated differently than older, vaccinated, clinically evaluable children.
  • In patients 0-28 days, full workup, IV antibiotics, and admission are generally appropriate (some variation allowed in 22-28 day group).
  • In patients 29-60 days, management depends on inflammatory markers and urinalysis.
  • Pediatric sepsis does not require hypotension for diagnosis and thus may be under-recognized.

Attending Pearls

  • Nothing magical happens at day 58 or 61; they still get UTIs and serious bacterial infections.
  • If well-appearing and unvaccinated, I often send blood culture but don't routinely LP.
  • They are more awake and accessible, giving more flexibility in this age group.
  • Vaccination status matters: clump with older kids if vaccinated, younger kids if unvaccinated/worried.
  • Pediatric sepsis does not require hypotension for diagnosis and thus may be under-recognized.

Pitfalls

  • Assuming a switch flips at 60 days; nothing magical happens.
  • Skipping urinalysis/urine culture in 2-month-old with bronchiolitis; UTI risk remains significant.
  • Treating all 60-90 day olds the same; vaccination status and clinical worry should guide workup.
  • Forgetting to send CSF for HSV if pleocytosis.
  • Giving antibiotics after LP when meningitis or sepsis is highly suspected; antibiotics should be prioritized before LP.
