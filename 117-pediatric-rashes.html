Pediatric Rashes

Dermatology · Week 50 · src: Approach to Pediatric Rashes, Approach to Pediatric Anemia
Bottom line: Categorize pediatric infectious rashes by morphology; maculopapular, vesicular, erythroderma, or petechial/purpuric.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Categorize rash as blanching maculopapular, vesicular, confluent erythematous, or petechial/purpuric.
↓
2Maculopapular with cough/coryza/conjunctivitis and high fever >39°C after 2-4 days: suspect measles; Koplik spots last 24-48 hours.
↓
3Slapped cheek then reticular lacy rash sparing palms/soles: suspect parvovirus; may have purpuric sock and glove syndrome.
↓
4Fever resolves then blanchable trunk rash 12-24 hours later: diagnose roseola (fever ~3 days).
↓
5Fever >5 days plus 4 of 5 CRASH criteria: consider Kawasaki; start workup.
↓
6Vesicular rash in different stages with low-grade fever: suspect varicella; <50 lesions if vaccinated; infectious until all scabbed.
↓
7Oral/hand/foot/butt lesions with fever: diagnose hand-foot-mouth (coxsackie); can be vesicles or red papules.
↓
8Giant blister with honey-crusted lesions: suspect bullous impetigo; treat with anti-staph antibiotic.
↓
9Sandpaper rash with strawberry tongue in 5-15yo: treat scarlet fever; check for circumoral pallor and Pastia lines.
↓
10Erythroderma with hypotension/multi-organ failure: treat toxic shock; Nikolsky-positive burn-like rash: treat SSSS.
↓
11Petechiae/purpura: sick/lethargic/cap refill >3s = meningococcemia; afebrile well = ITP; cranky stable = HSP.

📝 Key Facts

  • Measles: rash appears after 2-4 days of fever usually above 39°C with cough, coryza, conjunctivitis; Koplik spots last 24-48 hours.
  • Roseola: about 3 days of fever, then defervescence and 12-24 hours later blanchable maculopapular rash on trunk.
  • Kawasaki: fever >5 days plus 4 of 5 criteria; untreated 20-25% risk coronary aneurysm; rash can be anything except vesicular; age 3 months to 8 years.
  • Breakthrough varicella: vaccinated children have low-grade fever, <50 lesions, mixed stages; infectious until all lesions scabbed.
  • Scarlet fever: age 5-15 years, fever and sore throat then sandpaper rash for 3-4 days; Pastia lines and circumoral pallor.
  • Toxic shock: blood cultures often negative, especially staph (<5%); strep toxic shock has worse prognosis; may have hand/foot desquamation 1-2 weeks after onset.
  • Meningococcemia: rash may initially blanch or be maculopapular for first 24 hours; poor capillary refill can appear within 8 hours; may cause Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome.
  • HSP: often afebrile with history of viral URI 1-3 weeks prior; symmetric rash on lower extremities; check urine for red cells/protein and creatinine if positive.

Attending Pearls

  • Use rash morphology to narrow differential: maculopapular, vesicular, erythroderma, or petechial/purpuric.
  • Scarlet fever feels like sandpaper; Kawasaki kids are universally miserable, scarlet fever kids relatively happy.
  • Bullous impetigo patients are happy; staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome patients are painful and ill-appearing.
  • Petechiae/purpura: sick lethargic with abnormal vitals = meningococcemia; happy playful = ITP; cranky stable = HSP.
  • Toxic shock is a sunburn in a sick-looking kid; treat early, fluid resuscitate, and give antibiotics rapidly.

Pitfalls

  • Assuming all vesicular rashes are varicella; lesions all in same stage should raise concern for smallpox.
  • Missing meningococcemia because early rash is blanching/maculopapular; check capillary refill and watch progression.
  • Culturing bullae in staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome; cultures are negative, obtain blood/nasopharynx instead.
  • Treating scarlet fever without considering Kawasaki in a miserable child with rash, strawberry tongue, or desquamation.
