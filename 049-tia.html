TIA

Neurology · Week 18 · src: Transient Ischemic Attack
Bottom line: Transient deficit without infarction is unstable angina of brain; ~15% stroke in 3 months.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Check airway, breathing, circulation; O2 if SpO2 <94%, IV access, avoid hypotension.
↓
2Obtain point-of-care glucose; hypoglycemia is one of the greatest stroke/TIA mimics.
↓
3History: sudden onset, negative symptoms, focal deficit matching a vascular territory.
↓
4Perform CV and comprehensive neurologic exam (language, vision, strength, sensory, CNs, gait, reflexes).
↓
5Keep BP <220/120 mm Hg; if thrombolytic candidate <185/110 mm Hg; use nicardipine, labetalol, or clevidipine.
↓
6Obtain ECG, place on telemonitoring, and send CBC, BMP, coagulation studies.
↓
7Get noncontrast CT head then CTA head and neck; MRI as available is gold standard.
↓
8Arrange echocardiography as soon as feasible to evaluate cardioembolic source.
↓
9Calculate ABCD2 or Canadian TIA score; do not rely solely on score for disposition.
↓
10Admit or observe high-risk patients; initiate antiplatelets and neurology consultation.

📝 Key Facts

  • BP goal in acute ischemic stroke or TIA: <220/120 mm Hg; if thrombolytic candidate <185/110 mm Hg.
  • 30-50% of traditional TIAs show acute infarction on diffusion-weighted MRI.
  • TIA definition no longer uses the 24-hour time window because infarction timing varies.
  • ~15% of TIA patients have a stroke within 3 months; about half of those occur in first 48 hours.
  • Up to 50% of suspected TIAs are mimics; one 2017 paper found 18% misdiagnosis, commonly seizure or complex migraine.
  • TIA can affect the brain, spinal cord, or retina.
  • TSI (transient symptoms with infarction) means resolved symptoms but acute infarction on imaging and higher risk for future stroke.
  • ABCD2 score aids in stratifying TIA patients by severity to guide antiplatelet therapy.

Attending Pearls

  • TIA is unstable angina of the brain: something's about to happen.
  • Treat TIA and small stroke the same clinically for risk stratification and treatment.
  • Use the triad sudden, negative, and focal to support TIA diagnosis.
  • Diffusion-weighted MRI detects cellular death much earlier than CT, driving new definitions.
  • Positive symptoms (e.g., paresthesias) or global symptoms suggest an alternative diagnosis, not TIA.

Pitfalls

  • Relying on ABCD2 or Canadian TIA score alone to determine disposition.
  • Sending patients home without workup; historically TIA treated as a go-home diagnosis.
  • Missing hypoglycemia as a great stroke/TIA mimic; always check point-of-care glucose.
  • Not obtaining vascular imaging (CTA or MRA) to assess extracranial and intracranial arteries.
  • Assuming a normal noncontrast CT rules out TIA or stroke; overall yield is low.
