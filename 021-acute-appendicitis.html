Acute Appendicitis

Gastrointestinal · Week 11 · src: Appendicitis, Acute Diverticulitis
Bottom line: Appendicitis: most common abdominal surgical emergency; perforation suspect if fever >39.4°C or symptoms >24 h.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Place on cardiac monitor and continuous pulse oximetry; secure IV access.
↓
2If perforated appendix with sepsis/septic shock, resuscitate with IV fluids and vasopressors before intubation.
↓
3Continue resuscitation of unstable patients until transported to operating room.
↓
4Initiate antibiotics as soon as possible while preparing for operating room.
↓
5Emergency appendectomy indicated in unstable patients or evidence of perforation (pus/fecal material in peritoneum).
↓
6Consider perforated appendicitis if ill-appearing, fever >39.4°C, electrolyte abnormalities, dehydration, or symptoms >24 h.
↓
7Treat pain with IV opioid analgesia (morphine or hydromorphone); lower doses in children, elderly, opioid-naive.
↓
8CT with IV contrast is diagnostic modality of choice for nonpregnant adults.
↓
9Consider ultrasound or MRI in children or pregnant women.
↓
10Nondiagnostic imaging does not rule out appendicitis; appendectomy remains standard treatment.

📝 Key Facts

  • Acute appendicitis is the most common abdominal surgical emergency worldwide.
  • Classic RLQ pain, anorexia, nausea, and vomiting seen in fewer than 50% of patients.
  • Perforated/complicated appendicitis signs: ill appearance, fever >39.4°C (102.9°F), electrolyte abnormalities, dehydration, symptoms >24 h.
  • CT may not show any findings of perforation.
  • Simple/uncomplicated appendicitis has no clinical or radiographic signs of perforation; complicated indicates perforation and/or peritonitis.
  • CT with IV contrast is diagnostic modality of choice for nonpregnant adults; ultrasound or MRI for children or pregnant women.
  • Nondiagnostic imaging does not rule out acute appendicitis.
  • Despite evidence antibiotics alone may manage uncomplicated appendicitis, appendectomy is still standard treatment; unstable or perforated patients treated operatively.

Attending Pearls

  • It's easier to defend not ordering a test that wasn't indicated than defending not acting on an abnormal result, even minimally abnormal.
  • Perform a repeat abdominal exam before the patient leaves the ED to catch progression and obtain additional history.
  • Less than 2% of appendicitis cases rupture within 36 hours of symptom onset; follow-up more than 36-48 hours later is not fine.
  • Make sure the patient is aware of diagnostic uncertainty; document in aftercare instructions and medical decision-making note.
  • Involve family: tell them appendicitis is extremely unlikely but still possible; return immediately for increased pain, fevers, or vomiting, and for any pain after 8-12 hours.

Pitfalls

  • Classic presentation (RLQ pain, anorexia, nausea, vomiting) seen in fewer than 50%; relying on it misses cases.
  • Nondiagnostic imaging does not rule out appendicitis.
  • CT may not show findings of perforation.
  • Ruling out appendicitis without additional testing when signs/symptoms are consistent; failing to act on minimally abnormal WBC.
  • Discharging with follow-up longer than 36-48 hours; not informing patient of diagnostic uncertainty.
