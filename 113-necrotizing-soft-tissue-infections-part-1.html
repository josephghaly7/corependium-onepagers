Necrotizing Soft Tissue Infections Part 1

Dermatology · Week 49 · src: Skin and Soft Tissue Infections
Bottom line: NSTI is a clinical diagnosis; LRINEC ≥6 doesn't confirm, negative imaging doesn't rule out.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Call surgery immediately for operative exploration/debridement if NSTI suspected; give broad empiric antibiotics.
↓
2Treat sepsis with usual resuscitation; consider occult NSTI with careful exam, CT if needed.
↓
3Recognize NSTI broad term (necrosis) vs necrotizing fasciitis subtype (fascia/subcutaneous, may extend to muscle).
↓
4Suspect based on pain out of proportion, rapidly spreading erythema, tachycardia; exam often subtle early.
↓
5Don't rely on late findings: fluctuance, crepitus, necrotic bullae, decreased sensation — often absent.
↓
6Account for neuropathy (diabetes, vascular disease): pain may be absent but suspicious exam still warrants workup.
↓
7Calculate LRINEC (0-13, six criteria); if below 6 may help rule out, but if ≥6 doesn't confirm NSTI vs bad cellulitis.
↓
8If atypical or extent unclear, obtain CT with IV contrast; normal scan without edema/stranding makes NSTI unlikely.
↓
9If gas or true deep fascial involvement on CT, proceed to OR; if diagnosis clear, do not delay for imaging.
↓
10Consider bedside ultrasound/STAFF (subcutaneous thickening, air, fascial fluid) and compare to contralateral side.

📝 Key Facts

  • NSTI includes necrotizing cellulitis, Clostridial gas gangrene/myonecrosis, Streptococcal myositis, and streptococcal toxic shock syndrome.
  • Necrotizing fasciitis involves fascial and subcutaneous layers and may extend to muscle.
  • Swelling, pain, and erythema were most common chief complaints in ~80% of proven necrotizing fasciitis cases.
  • Bullae, overlying skin necrosis, and crepitus found in <25% of cases; fever and tachycardia present in <60% of patients.
  • Pain out of proportion to exam should raise suspicion; patients with poorly controlled diabetes or vascular disease at higher risk.
  • LRINEC score: 0-13 based on 6 criteria (CRP, WBC, hemoglobin, sodium, creatinine, glucose); initial 2004 study sensitivity 90% and specificity 95% for ≥6.
  • 2018 meta-analysis pooled sensitivity 68% and specificity 85% at cutoff 6; case reports of OR-confirmed necrotizing fasciitis with score 0.
  • Hypotension is uncommon and late, but if present strongly correlates with multiorgan failure and death.

Attending Pearls

  • Pain out of proportion to exam should bring necrotizing fasciitis to mind.
  • A completely negative CT scan (no soft tissue edema or stranding) makes NSTI very unlikely.
  • Gas within soft tissues on CT is all but certain for necrotizing infection.
  • Necrotizing infection is a clinical diagnosis; if clear, the patient needs the OR, resuscitation, and antibiotics.

Pitfalls

  • Relying on classic late findings (fluctuance, crepitus, bullae, necrosis) to diagnose; early presentations are subtle.
  • Using a normal LRINEC score to rule out disease; scores of zero have occurred with OR-confirmed necrotizing fasciitis.
  • Treating a completely normal CT as exclusion only if no soft tissue edema or stranding; any uncertainty requires clinical judgment.
  • Assuming all patients have severe pain; diabetics and vascular disease patients may have neuropathy and less pain.
  • Using imaging in isolation to rule out NSTI; it is only a part of the overall clinical picture.
