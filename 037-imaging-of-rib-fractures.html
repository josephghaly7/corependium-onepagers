Imaging of Rib Fractures

Trauma · Week 15 · src: Blunt Thoracic Trauma
Bottom line: CXR misses about two-thirds of rib fractures; those missed on CT don't change mortality.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1If patient looks well, comfortable, breathing fine with good story, consider no imaging; treat symptomatically.
↓
2Otherwise, start with PA and lateral chest x-ray for suspected rib fracture.
↓
3If ribs 1 or 2 fractured, get chest CT with IV contrast/angiogram to evaluate great vessels.
↓
4For lower rib fractures, perform E-FAST to check for intra-abdominal bleeding.
↓
5Use NEXUS chest decision aids sequentially to decide if CXR and CT are needed.
↓
6If abnormal CXR shows clavicle fracture, widened mediastinum, or anything beyond a single rib fracture, get chest CT.
↓
7If distracting injury (eg, broken femur) or chest wall/sternum/thoracic spine/scapular tenderness, get chest CT.
↓
8If rapid deceleration (fall >20 ft or MVA >40 mph), get chest CT.
↓
9If isolated single rib fracture without high-risk criteria, skip CT; give pain meds and incentive spirometry.
↓
10If solo coverage and want more comfort, consider adding rib series; evidence limited, adds ~1 mSv.

📝 Key Facts

  • Chest x-ray misses about two-thirds of rib fractures identified on chest CT.
  • Rib fractures seen only on CT do not increase mortality compared with fractures visible on both CXR and CT.
  • Fractures of ribs 1 or 2 double the chance of great vessel injury; obtain CT angiogram of great vessels and neck vessels.
  • NEXUS chest CT criteria: abnormal CXR with clavicle fracture or widened mediastinum, anything beyond a single rib fracture, distracting injury, chest wall/sternum/thoracic spine/scapular tenderness, fall >20 ft, or MVA >40 mph.
  • If it is a bad enough fracture to cause increased morbidity or mortality, the CXR will show something (fracture, pulmonary contusion, or other sign of serious injury).
  • Rib series adds ~1 mSv (1 mSv for CXR to 2 mSv with additional views; three views unilateral, six views bilateral) but doesn't find more fractures than PA/lateral when read by radiologists.
  • Ultra-low-dose chest CT protocols deliver ~2-2.5 mSv and can detect bony injury like rib fractures.
  • Ultrasound is highly sensitive for rib fractures in trained hands but is exceptionally operator- and patient-habitus dependent.

Attending Pearls

  • Treat rib contusions and small fractures the same: pain meds, incentive spirometry, return precautions.
  • If you can avoid the CT altogether, just get a chest X-ray or no imaging at all in some patients; don't tie up the CT scanner just because you can.
  • Ultrasound is fantastic; there's a role for it in just about anything.
  • Rib series may be reasonable for solo overnight coverage to increase comfort; radiation increase is small and incidental.
  • If CT is needed, ultra-low-dose protocols matter a lot when you're going to get the CT anyway; work with radiologists to protocolize.

Pitfalls

  • Don't tell patients a normal CXR rules out rib fracture; about two-thirds are missed.
  • Don't obtain chest CT for every rib fracture; isolated single fracture without high-risk criteria doesn't need it.
  • Don't rely on rib series to find significantly more fractures; evidence shows little added yield.
  • Don't use ultrasound to definitively diagnose rib fractures unless highly trained; costochondral junctions and contusions can look like fractures (false positives).
  • Don't assume lower rib fractures are only a chest problem; assess for concurrent intra-abdominal injury with E-FAST.
