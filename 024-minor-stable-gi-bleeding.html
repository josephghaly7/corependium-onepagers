Minor/Stable GI Bleeding

Gastrointestinal · Week 12 · src: Upper Gastrointestinal Bleeding, Lower Gastrointestinal Bleeding
Bottom line: 5-10% of GI bleeds are massive; most stop spontaneously (80%), focus on variceal/ulcer risk.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Assess ABCs, place on monitor, IV access, decide stable vs unstable (massive vs minor).
↓
2If unstable, follow massive GI bleed algorithm (not covered here).
↓
3If stable, consider non-GI sources (vaginal, urinary, hemoptysis, epistaxis).
↓
4Perform focused history: prior scope, alcohol, NSAIDs, anticoagulants, steroids, comorbidities.
↓
5Perform focused physical: vital signs, signs of liver disease/portal hypertension, abdominal exam for peritonitis, rectal exam for melena/hematochezia.
↓
6Obtain labs: hematocrit/hemoglobin (serial), CBC, PT/INR (no PTT), type and screen, BUN, chemistry.
↓
7Stratify risk using Glasgow-Blatchford or Rockall score (0 low risk, ≥6 high risk).
↓
8If high risk (varices concern, anticoagulants, comorbidities, melena, syncope, CHF), admit for endoscopy.
↓
9If low risk (young, no comorbidities, normal vitals, no melena), consider discharge with PPI and outpatient GI follow-up within 24-72 hours.
↓
10If active hematemesis/melena/hematochezia, endoscopy today (ED or GI suite).

Outpatient GI follow-up scopingwithin 24-72 hoursOnly if hospital/urgent care policy allows; otherwise admit.

📝 Key Facts

  • Massive GI bleed is 5-10% of all GI bleeds.
  • 80% of GI bleeds stop spontaneously; 20% rebleed.
  • Melena is considered an unstable vital sign.
  • Glasgow-Blatchford score includes BUN, hemoglobin, blood pressure, heart rate, melena, syncope, hepatic disease, heart failure; score 0 = low risk, ≥6 = 50% chance of intervention or bad outcome.
  • Rockall score adds age to Glasgow-Blatchford.
  • NG tube is not recommended in stable patients (painful, inaccurate).
  • Reverse anticoagulation in unstable bleeding; stable patients with INR 1.3-1.5 may not need reversal before endoscopy.
  • For CHF patients, avoid fluid boluses; transfuse blood sooner, consider furosemide between units.

Attending Pearls

  • Melena equals unstable equals you're going to stay.
  • The patient with vague complaints that might have something bad or nothing, that scares the crap out of me.
  • It's the bleed that sort of leads to one of those things being exacerbated.
  • If there's a concern about esophageal varices, then you're going to be erring towards admitting them because they might actually look stable and they might die of a GI bleed in the next day.
  • GI bleed, fortunately, like a bad cellulitis will represent. They'll come back. So I'm not worried about them going home to die.

Pitfalls

  • Assuming all hematochezia is from a lower GI source (brisk upper GI bleed can present with bright red stools).
  • Using an NG tube in stable patients to differentiate upper vs lower GI bleed (inaccurate, painful).
  • Giving large fluid boluses to CHF patients with GI bleed (worsens heart failure).
  • Discharging patients with melena, syncope, or high-risk features (anticoagulants, varices, comorbidities).
  • Relying solely on hemoglobin/hematocrit; serial measurements needed to detect ongoing bleeding.
