Pediatric Stridor Part 1

Additional Content · src: Croup
Bottom line: Croup accounts for >90% of pediatric stridor; racemic epinephrine is both diagnostic and therapeutic.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Assess severity (retractions, respiratory effort, O2 sat), progression, feeding, cyanosis/apnea, sleep (SPECS).
↓
2Take history focusing on chronicity and trajectory, age, feeding, weight gain, and sleep.
↓
3For stridor at rest, start racemic epinephrine 2.25% 0.05-0.1 mL/kg nebulized q15-20 min prn (max 0.5 mL).
↓
4Give dexamethasone 0.15-0.6 mg/kg PO, IV, or IM once (max 16 mg).
↓
5If child cannot tolerate PO or has no IV access, use nebulized budesonide (non-inferior).
↓
6Trial feeding: inability to drink/breastfeed predicts decompensation; hypoxia mandates admission.
↓
7Use racemic epinephrine response as diagnostic: improvement suggests croup; no response suggests foreign body or structural cause.
↓
8If croup suspected, treat; if no cough, toxic appearance, or poor response, consider bacterial tracheitis, foreign body, epiglottitis.
↓
9For well-appearing chronic stridor with good feeding, reassure and arrange outpatient ENT follow-up.
↓
10Admit for hypoxia, distress, retractions, toxic appearance, poor feeding, or suspected foreign body.

📝 Key Facts

  • Croup accounts for >90% of stridor in children.
  • Croup most commonly occurs in children 6 months to 3 years old.
  • Laryngomalacia symptoms peak at 6-8 months and resolve by 12-24 months; onset is shortly after birth.
  • Infant airway at 3-4 months is approximately 4 mm in diameter.
  • 95% of all cardiac arrests in children have a respiratory etiology.
  • Hypoxia with stridor mandates admission; benign stridor should have normal oxygen saturation.
  • SPECS mnemonic: Severity, Progression, Eating difficulties, Cyanosis/apnea, Sleep disturbances.
  • Stridor without cough is more concerning for foreign body or structural lesion; croup typically has a barky cough.

Attending Pearls

  • Ask about feeding and weight gain first; good feeding/weight gain is the most reassuring branch point.
  • Stridor is distressing noise but not equivalent to respiratory distress; distinguish noise from true work of breathing.
  • Prone positioning or neck extension can temporarily improve laryngomalacia stridor, but not a home treatment.
  • Chronic stridor plus a viral URI sounds worse; older infants usually tolerate if feeding and wet diapers remain good.
  • Racemic epinephrine can be used as a diagnostic test: improvement supports croup, no response suggests foreign body.

Pitfalls

  • Equating stridor with croup and missing chronic anatomic causes like laryngomalacia, tracheomalacia, or subglottic stenosis.
  • Sending home a child with stridor plus retractions, hypoxia, poor feeding, or toxic appearance.
  • Assuming chronic stridor is always benign; hemangiomas/cysts can grow and worsen, and viral illness can acutely decompensate.
  • Reassuring laryngomalacia without checking weight gain or feeding; failure to thrive is a red flag.
  • Using antibiotics for croup unless bacterial tracheitis, epiglottitis, or retropharyngeal cellulitis/abscess is suspected.
