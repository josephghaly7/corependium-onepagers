Severe Alcohol Withdrawal

Toxicology · Week 38 · src: CorePendium Ethanol Intoxication, Toxicity, and Poisoning, Ethanol Withdrawal
Bottom line: Severe alcohol withdrawal: lead with phenobarb 10 mg/kg IV, 130 mg q15min to 20 mg/kg; diazepam if benzo.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Confirm severe withdrawal (DTs/pre-DTs); rule out mimics; no phenobarb contraindications.
↓
2If using phenobarb: 10 mg/kg IBW IV over 30 minutes; then wait 15 minutes.
↓
3Give phenobarb 130 mg IV every 15 min until calm/arousable or cumulative 20 mg/kg.
↓
4At 20 mg/kg (soft stop), reassess for alternative diagnosis (e.g., hepatic encephalopathy).
↓
5If truly withdrawal, may push to 30 mg/kg cumulative (hard stop).
↓
6If uncontrolled at 30 mg/kg, intubate and start propofol infusion; consider adding dexmedetomidine at propofol 80 mcg/kg/min.
↓
7If using diazepam: 10 mg IV, repeat every 10 min; then 20 mg x2, then 40 mg until calm/arousable.
↓
8Stop diazepam at 200 mg total; may give 50-100 mg boluses or switch to direct GABA agent.
↓
9If naive to phenobarb, start in known complicated withdrawal patients; use diazepam otherwise.
↓
10Avoid adding phenobarb after massive benzodiazepine doses due oversedation risk.

📝 Key Facts

  • Phenobarb loading dose: 10 mg/kg IBW IV over 30 min; may repeat 130 mg q15min; 20 mg/kg soft stop; 30 mg/kg hard stop.
  • Diazepam protocol: start 10 mg IV, repeat q10min; up to 20 mg then 40 mg; total 200 mg cutoff.
  • Phenobarb 20 mg/kg: pause and reassess; if still withdrawal, can push to 30 mg/kg.
  • Benzodiazepines are indirect GABA agonists; phenobarb and propofol are direct; benzos may fail in some patients.
  • Phenobarb contraindications: unclear diagnosis, hepatic encephalopathy, interactions with HIV meds.
  • Titration target: RASS 0 to -1 (calm, sleepy but arousable), not heart rate.
  • Diazepam preferred over lorazepam due rapid onset and long half-life; lorazepam effect may take 15 minutes.
  • High-dose propofol for refractory withdrawal: 100-150 mcg/kg/min; add dexmedetomidine at 80 mcg/kg/min propofol to reduce PRIS risk.

Attending Pearls

  • Phenobarb is cleaner as a solo agent; lead with it early in severe withdrawal to avoid intubation.
  • At 20 mg/kg phenobarb, pause and ask if the diagnosis is correct; 30 mg/kg should work if truly alcohol withdrawal.
  • Diazepam's long half-life protects patients throughout hospitalization; short-acting lorazepam may lead to underdosing on the floor.
  • Dexmedetomidine counters the hyperadrenergic state but is evidence-free; use as adjunct to high-dose propofol.
  • No DT patient should die with a properly dosed regimen of either benzos or barbiturates.

Pitfalls

  • Titrating to heart rate instead of sedation target (RASS 0 to -1); tachycardia may indicate another cause.
  • Adding phenobarbital after massive benzodiazepine doses causes oversedation and may lead to intubation.
  • Using antipsychotics or ketamine for pure withdrawal masks agitation as a titration marker; risk of hemodynamic instability.
  • Discharging on phenobarbital without follow-up; speakers do not discharge these patients.
  • Failure to anticipate airway compromise and monitor airway early.
