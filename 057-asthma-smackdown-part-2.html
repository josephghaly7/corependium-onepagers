Asthma Smackdown: Part 2

Pediatrics · Week 23 · src: Asthma in Adults
Bottom line: Intubate tired pediatric asthmatics early; ketamine 1.5-2 mg/kg, vent 5-6 mL/kg, I:E 1:4, plateau ≤30, avoid bicarb.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Check ABG/ETCO2 10-20 min after BiPAP; if PCO2 not improving, consider intubation.
↓
2If child tiring, mental status declining, and not turning after kitchen sink, intubate before reserve exhausted.
↓
3Give IV fluid bolus before intubation for preload; then induce with ketamine 1.5-2 mg/kg IV.
↓
4Consider laryngoscopy after bagging; if needed give paralytic, use cuffed ET tube, video laryngoscopy.
↓
5After intubation, disconnect vent/BVM and manually compress chest to expel trapped air.
↓
6Set vent: 5-6 mL/kg tidal volume, slow rate, I:E 1:4, plateau ≤30; allow permissive hypercapnia.
↓
7If deterioration after intubation, get portable CXR for pneumothorax; avoid bicarb unless pH <6.9 with adequate vent.
↓
8Give inhaled anesthetics if available for refractory bronchospasm; heliox not useful if FiO2 >30%.
↓
9Keep paralyzed with rocuronium if needed for exhalation; sedate with ketamine boluses; avoid barotrauma.

⏱ Time Targets

Reassess after BiPAP10-20 minutesCheck ABG or ETCO2 to see if PCO2 improving; if not, intubate

💊 Doses

ketamine1.5-2 mg/kg IVInduction for intubation; avoid sub-dissociative dosing; may use 1 mg/kg for look-see but 2 mg/kg needed for intubation

📝 Key Facts

  • Community ED without PICU: intubate when child is tiring, mental status declining, and not turning after maximal therapy.
  • Risk of intubation rises exponentially: pH 7.2/O2 sat 85 vs pH 6.9/PCO2 90/O2 sat 70; earlier intubation safer.
  • BiPAP reduces intubation need; give it time but reassess at 10-20 minutes; ETCO2 trend may help but can falsely improve as child tires.
  • Vent settings: 5-6 mL/kg tidal volume, I:E 1:4, plateau pressure max 30, slow respiratory rate, permissive hypercapnia.
  • Bicarb generates CO2; if patient cannot exhale, it worsens acidosis; avoid unless pH <6.9 as temporizing with adequate ventilation.
  • After intubation, manual chest compression expels trapped air and improves venous return; use a cuffed ET tube even in small children.
  • Heliox loses benefit if FiO2 >30%; most intubated asthmatics need higher FiO2, so not useful.
  • Ketamine as asthma treatment at lower sub-dissociative doses is not effective; only use for induction.

Attending Pearls

  • Use the 'I've seen this movie before' rule: once downward trajectory continues despite maximal therapy, intubate.
  • Manual chest compression after intubation expels trapped air and improves venous return to the heart.
  • Use a cuffed ET tube even in small children; essential for ventilator management.
  • Heliox not useful when FiO2 >30% needed; inhaled anesthetics may help but often unavailable.
  • Ketamine is the only sedative choice for induction; paralytic usually needed for successful intubation.

Pitfalls

  • Intubating too late after physiologic reserve exhausted; risk of peri-intubation arrest rises exponentially.
  • Bagging freshly intubated asthmatic at 25-30 breaths/min causes fatal air trapping and hypotension.
  • Giving sodium bicarbonate when patient cannot exhale CO2 causes paradoxical acidosis.
  • Relying on sub-dissociative ketamine or ketamine alone without paralytic when intubation truly needed.
  • Forgetting to manually decompress chest after intubation; stacked breaths worsen hypotension.
