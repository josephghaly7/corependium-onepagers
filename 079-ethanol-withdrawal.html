Ethanol Withdrawal

Toxicology · Week 37 · src: Ethanol Withdrawal
Bottom line: Severe ethanol withdrawal needs massive sedation, supportive care, and early airway control; can be fatal.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Assess ABCs, IV access, oxygen, monitor; intubate if airway cannot be protected.
↓
2Give oxygen to keep SpO2 >92%; ABG as needed; nasal cannula preferred to avoid aspiration.
↓
3Check glucose; give dextrose 50% 50 mL (25 g) IV via large vein if hypoglycemic.
↓
4Give Ringer lactate boluses for hypotension or dehydration; consider IV thiamine for wet beriberi.
↓
5Start diazepam 10 mg IV, double every 15-20 min until symptom control (or lorazepam 2-4 mg IV, or midazolam 4-8 mg IV every 10-15 min).
↓
6If benzodiazepine-refractory or confirmed withdrawal, use phenobarbital 10 mg/kg IV over 15-30 min, or 130-260 mg IV every 15-60 min until control.
↓
7For persistent agitation/hallucinosis despite benzodiazepines or phenobarbital, consider antipsychotics (eg, haloperidol, droperidol); avoid olanzapine with benzodiazepines.
↓
8Treat nausea/vomiting with antiemetics (eg, ondansetron).
↓
9For alcohol ketoacidosis with persistent acidemia, treat with IV dextrose and, when appropriate, oral complex carbohydrates.
↓
10If intubating, use GABAergic induction (benzodiazepines, propofol); avoid succinylcholine if hyperkalemia or prolonged seizure concern; post-intubation sedate with propofol + fentanyl, phenobarbital, or ketamine; consider propofol drip.

📝 Key Facts

  • Severe ethanol withdrawal can be fatal if not recognized and treated promptly.
  • Patients may have measurable or elevated serum ethanol concentrations yet still withdraw; they often have more severe withdrawal and may require both a benzodiazepine and phenobarbital.
  • Ethanol withdrawal syndrome, including delirium tremens, is a clinical diagnosis based on history and physical exam.
  • CIWA-Ar and SEWS are validated scoring tools but should be used as part of clinical assessment, not rigid protocol-driven tools.
  • Delirium tremens is defined as severe alcohol withdrawal with alteration in ability to interact with environment, plus cognitive perceptual disturbances not due to another condition.
  • Common pitfalls: inadequate treatment with benzodiazepines and/or phenobarbital, and failure to anticipate and monitor airway compromise early.
  • RSI: avoid succinylcholine if concern for hyperkalemia from prolonged seizure activity; post-intubation sedation options include propofol with fentanyl, phenobarbital, or ketamine.
  • Olanzapine with benzodiazepines is generally not recommended due to increased respiratory depression risk (boxed warning).

Attending Pearls

  • These patients can be unbelievably ill; treatment is an enormous amount of sedation—drugs in doses you can't imagine—plus supportive care.
  • This is a truly life-threatening disorder that you have to get ahead of.
  • You give them until the patient is calmed; don't worry about usual medication doses.
  • If you have to take their airway, sometimes you have to take their airway.
  • Always go back to ABC, IV, O2, monitor when a patient is crashing.

Pitfalls

  • Under-treatment with benzodiazepines and/or phenobarbital is a common pitfall.
  • Failure to anticipate airway compromise and monitor the airway early.
  • Stopping sedation because doses seem excessive; patients with severe withdrawal may tolerate massive doses.
  • Using olanzapine with benzodiazepines when possible, due to increased risk of respiratory depression (boxed warning).
  • Not considering alternative diagnoses (trauma, infection, other causes of altered mental status) in patients with ethanol withdrawal.
