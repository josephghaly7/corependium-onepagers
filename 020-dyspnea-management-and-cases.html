Dyspnea: Management and Cases

Pulmonary · Week 10 · src: Approach to Acute Dyspnea and Respiratory Distress
Bottom line: 90% of dyspnea is cardiopulmonary; treat empirically while diagnosing, avoiding intubation in COPD/asthma.

📝 Key Facts

  • Acute dyspnea accounts for nearly 4 million ED visits annually; most common in >65 years.
  • 90% of dyspnea cases are due to cardiac or pulmonary causes.
  • Red flags: HR >140 or <40 suggests primary cardiac issue needing ACLS algorithm.
  • Normal or low BP in a distressed dyspneic patient is a red flag.
  • S3 heart sound is very specific for congestive heart failure.
  • Clear lungs in a severely dyspneic patient suggests PE, MI, or cardiac tamponade.
  • BNP can be elevated in PE and other conditions, not 100% specific for CHF.
  • For pneumothorax >20% or struggling patient, needle/chest tube needed.

Attending Pearls

  • If the patient looks sick, is very short of breath, but has clear lungs, think PE-MI-cardiac tamponade.
  • Intubating a COPD exacerbation or asthmatic is literally the last thing you want to do.
  • Oxygen in COPD: aim for SpO2 ~92%, not 100%.

Pitfalls

  • Forcing a dyspneic patient to lie supine instead of allowing position of comfort.
  • Delaying BiPAP until patient tires out; early administration prevents intubation.
  • Over-relying on BNP for CHF diagnosis; it can be elevated in PE.
  • Assuming clear lungs rule out serious cause; consider PE, MI, tamponade.
  • Intubating COPD/asthma patients without trying BiPAP first; increases mortality.
