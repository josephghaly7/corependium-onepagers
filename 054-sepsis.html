Sepsis

Infectious Disease · Week 20 · 16 min
Bottom line: Recognize sepsis early, resuscitate early, give early antibiotics, pursue source control.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Identify sepsis early using Sepsis-3: life-threatening organ dysfunction from dysregulated host response to infection.
↓
2Assess for shock using hypoperfusion markers, not just blood pressure: mental status, skin, cap refill, urine output, lactate, bicarb.
↓
3If sepsis is moderately high on differential for shock, give one empiric broad-spectrum antibiotic dose early.
↓
4Do not delay antibiotics to identify the source; give initial dose before full workup.
↓
5Do not automatically give a fluid bolus; take time to determine if shock is truly septic.
↓
6If septic shock confirmed (hypotension plus lactate >2 despite resuscitation), treat early with fluids and antibiotics.
↓
7Pursue early source control: antibiotics plus drainage or other interventions as needed.
↓
8Reassess for end-organ dysfunction and response using creatinine, LFTs, and perfusion markers.

📝 Key Facts

  • Sepsis-3 defines sepsis as life-threatening organ dysfunction due to dysregulated host response to infection.
  • Septic shock is sepsis plus persistent hypotension and lactate greater than 2 despite volume resuscitation.
  • Fever, tachycardia, and elevated white count are normal physiologic responses to infection, not sepsis.
  • Shock is hypoperfusion, not hypotension; compensated shock can present with hypertension, tachycardia, and tachypnea.
  • CMS SEP-1 core measures, published 2015, follow Sepsis-2 definitions and tie reimbursement to 3-hour and 6-hour bundles.
  • In septic shock, early antibiotics are not controversial; fluid resuscitation is controversial due to weak evidence.

Attending Pearls

  • Early recognition and resuscitation improve mortality; treat sepsis with the same urgency as stroke or STEMI.
  • Give a single empiric broad-spectrum antibiotic dose once sepsis crosses your mind; you cannot turn back the clock.
  • Think early source control, not just antibiotics; an obstructed infected stone needs drainage too.
  • Shock is about hypoperfusion, not blood pressure; use multiple markers to detect compensated shock.

Pitfalls

  • Conflating sepsis, shock, and hypotension; missing compensated shock because blood pressure is normal or high.
  • Delaying antibiotics to first identify the source; sicker patients need immediate empiric dosing.
  • Automatically pairing antibiotics with a fluid bolus before confirming shock is septic, potentially harming cardiogenic shock patients.
  • Labeling every high-lactate hypotensive patient as septic without considering other shock types.
