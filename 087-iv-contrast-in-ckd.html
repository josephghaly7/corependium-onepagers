087

IV Contrast in CKD

Renal and Urogenital · Week 40 · 7 min
Bottom line: Don't withhold IV contrast for life/limb-threatening diagnosis regardless of GFR; risk overstated.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Assess whether CT with IV contrast is needed for limb- or life-threatening diagnosis.
↓
2If emergency and contrast needed, do not delay for creatinine, GFR, or prophylaxis.
↓
3If GFR greater than or equal to 30, give contrast when necessary without delay or prophylaxis.
↓
4If GFR less than 30 and not dialysis-dependent, consider crystalloid prophylaxis if no volume overload.
↓
5If volume overload signs present, do not give volume expansion prophylaxis.
↓
6If emergency and GFR <30, don't delay CT for prophylaxis; give volume after.
↓
7If dialysis-dependent but still making urine, treat like GFR less than 30.
↓
8If single kidney, no increased risk; no special precautions needed.
↓
9If non-emergent and GFR less than 30, clinician determines contrast based on diagnostic need.

📝 Key Facts

  • ACR 2017: no clear threshold above which contrast can't be used; little evidence IV contrast independently causes AKI if GFR >=30.
  • Creatinine screening before IV contrast only needed if age >60, renal disease, hypertension, diabetes, or metformin use.
  • Anuric ESRD patients do not need immediate dialysis after contrast administration.
  • Risk of contrast-induced AKI: ~0% if GFR >45, 0-2% if GFR 30-45, 0-17% if GFR <30.
  • Contrast-associated AKI is AKI within 48 hours of contrast, implying correlation not causation; contrast-induced AKI implies causation.
  • January 2020 consensus statement endorsed by ACR and National Kidney Foundation states risk of AKI after IV contrast has been overstated.

Attending Pearls

  • If the patient needs contrast to make the diagnosis, they should get it.
  • Most AKI after contrast is contrast-associated, not contrast-induced.
  • Use the ACR guideline and this consensus statement to create ED contrast administration recommendations.
  • The key is to make the diagnosis immediately so the patient can be treated appropriately.

Pitfalls

  • Delaying or withholding contrast in emergencies can cause indirect harm from delayed diagnosis and misdiagnosis.
  • Assuming contrast caused AKI when it occurs within 48 hours; underlying illness is often the culprit.
  • Giving bicarbonate for prophylaxis; no benefit over normal saline or other crystalloid.
  • Giving volume expansion prophylaxis to a patient with signs or symptoms of volume overload.
