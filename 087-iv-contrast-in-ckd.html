IV Contrast in CKD

Renal and Urogenital · Week 40 · src: CorePendium Acute Kidney Injury, Contrast-Induced Nephropathy
Bottom line: Risk of IV contrast AKI overstated; never delay contrast for life/limb-threatening diagnosis, GFR >=30 risk ~0%.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Assess whether CT with IV contrast is needed for a limb- or life-threatening diagnosis.
↓
2If emergency indication, give contrast when needed; do not delay for creatinine, GFR, or prophylaxis.
↓
3If GFR >=30, give contrast when necessary without delay or any other considerations.
↓
4If GFR <30 and not on maintenance dialysis, consider crystalloid volume expansion prophylaxis.
↓
5If volume overload signs/symptoms present, do not give volume expansion prophylaxis.
↓
6If emergency and GFR <30, don't delay CT for pre-procedural prophylaxis; give volume after (post-procedural).
↓
7If dialysis-dependent but still makes urine, treat like GFR <30.
↓
8If single kidney, no increased risk; no special precautions needed.
↓
9If non-emergent and GFR <30, clinician determines contrast based on diagnostic need; if needed, give it.

📝 Key Facts

  • ACR 2017: no clear threshold above which contrast can't be used; little evidence IV contrast independently causes AKI if GFR >=30.
  • ACR 2017: creatinine screening before IV contrast only needed if age >60, renal disease, hypertension, diabetes, or metformin use.
  • ACR 2017: anuric ESRD patients do not need immediate dialysis after contrast.
  • January 2020 Radiology consensus statement endorsed by ACR and National Kidney Foundation: risk of AKI after IV contrast has been overstated.
  • Risk of contrast-induced AKI: ~0% if GFR >45, 0-2% if GFR 30-45, 0-17% if GFR <30.
  • Contrast-associated AKI = any AKI within 48 hours of contrast (correlation, not causation); contrast-induced AKI = AKI results from contrast (causation).
  • Prophylaxis recommendation only if GFR <30 and not on maintenance dialysis, with minimal supporting data; not recommended if GFR >30.
  • No benefit of bicarbonate over normal saline or any other crystalloid for prophylaxis.
  • Patients with a single kidney are at no particular increased risk for contrast-induced or contrast-associated AKI.

Attending Pearls

  • If the patient needs contrast to make the diagnosis, they should get it.
  • Most AKI after contrast is contrast-associated, not contrast-induced.
  • If the study is an emergency, don't delay the CT and give the contrast when it's needed to make the diagnosis.
  • The key is to make the diagnosis immediately so we can treat the patient appropriately.
  • Use the ACR guideline and this consensus statement to create ED contrast administration recommendations.

Pitfalls

  • Delaying or withholding contrast in emergencies can cause indirect harm from delayed diagnosis and misdiagnosis.
  • Assuming contrast caused AKI when it occurs within 48 hours; underlying illness is often the culprit.
  • Giving bicarbonate for prophylaxis; no benefit over normal saline or other crystalloid.
  • Giving volume expansion prophylaxis to a patient with signs or symptoms of volume overload.
  • Withholding contrast from dialysis-dependent patients who still make urine; treat them like GFR <30.
