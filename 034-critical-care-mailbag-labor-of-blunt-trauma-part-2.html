Critical Care Mailbag: Labor of Blunt Trauma Part 2

Trauma · Week 15 · src: Blunt Abdominal Trauma, Blunt Thoracic Trauma
Bottom line: Trauma leader: POC calcium/lactate, trochanteric binder, CT with blood, selective thoracotomy.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1If sending labs to lab, send only type and screen; POC labs check ionized calcium and lactate.
↓
2If chest tube placed, personally palpate lung to rule out chest as decompensation source.
↓
3If peri-arrest blunt trauma, empirically bind pelvis; tuck on greater trochanters and tape feet.
↓
4If not peri-arrest, get pelvic X-ray before deciding to bind.
↓
5For venous access in sick trauma, prefer subclavian; femoral acceptable unless ortho/IR working pelvis.
↓
6If head-injured, keep PaCO2 35-40; if EtCO2 >40, increase vent rate; if herniation, give 3% hypertonic saline or 7% sodium bicarbonate amp.
↓
7For thoracotomy: if no cardiac motion and no effusion, stop; if effusion, open and drain.
↓
8If penetrating stab with recent arrest and heart motion, open chest even in single-doc shop.
↓
9For blunt trauma not witnessed arrest, don't do thoracotomy.
↓
10At 15 minutes decide destination; don't stay >30 minutes; bring full team and blood to CT.

📝 Key Facts

  • Ionized calcium is most missed and important POC lab; under-resuscitating on calcium begets coagulopathy of trauma and has hemodynamic consequences.
  • All base excess, base deficit, lactate, and pH are lactate; lactate is the root cause.
  • Pelvic binder is really a trochanteric binder; should be on greater trochanters, not covering femoral site.
  • PaCO2 maintained 35-40; EtCO2 only helps if above 40 because shock causes low EtCO2.
  • Most blunt thoracic trauma is non-operative and managed conservatively; MVC most common cause.
  • Blunt thoracic trauma may need breathing addressed before airway (decompress tension pneumothorax before intubation).
  • Older patients have elevated risk of morbidity and mortality in blunt thoracic trauma.
  • Absence of abdominal pain does not exclude significant abdominal injury (negative likelihood ratio 0.52, 95% CI 0.34-0.79).

Attending Pearls

  • Dotted red line card: things you think about but won't do yourself; if you do the task you can't be trauma team leader.
  • I'm not satisfied unless I've put my finger in the hole and touched lung; rule out chest as source in 10 seconds.
  • Quiet resuscitation, the quiet room has a lot of power; better for patient and team management.
  • If blunt trauma and they didn't die in front of you and you don't want to do thoracotomy, don't do it—you're done.
  • Subclavian is the only site acceptable to do blind; ultrasound-guided subclavian is a viable strategy for trauma.

Pitfalls

  • Sending comprehensive lab panel to central lab; results outdated and useless by return time.
  • Sticking radial artery for ABG before CT when VBG suffices; wastes time.
  • Placing pelvic binder too high, covering femoral access sites; should be on greater trochanters.
  • Leaving feet externally rotated without taping together, preventing full pelvic binding.
  • Sending patient to CT with junior resident and no equipment; CT is where patients die.
