DKA and HHS

Endocrine · Week 45 · src: Diabetic Ketoacidosis
Bottom line: DKA/HHS: avoid intubation, fluid resuscitate, check potassium, start insulin only after K ≥3.5.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1ABCs: monitor, pulse oximetry, IV access (avoid intubation if possible).
↓
2Resuscitate with crystalloid before insulin; if shock, 1–1.5 L bolus.
↓
3Check potassium before insulin: if >5.0 start insulin; 3.5–5.0 insulin + KCl; <3.5 give K+ first.
↓
4Start regular insulin 0.1 unit/kg/hr IV infusion; consider 0.1 unit/kg SC/IM bolus if delayed.
↓
5Add 5%–10% dextrose to IV fluid if glucose <250 mg/dL before acidosis corrects.
↓
6Give sodium bicarbonate only if pH <7.0, K >6.5, or profound shock unresponsive to fluids.
↓
7If suspected cerebral edema: stop fluids, elevate head, give mannitol or 3% saline.
↓
8Recheck fingerstick glucose every 1–2 hours and serum electrolytes every 2–4 hours.
↓
9Search for triggers (infection, insulin non-adherence, ischemia) and obtain additional labs/imaging.

📝 Key Facts

  • DKA diagnosis: glucose >200 mg/dL or established diabetes, pH <7.3 or HCO3 <18 mEq/L, and ketones (β-hydroxybutyrate >3.0 mmol/L preferred, or urine ketones 2+).
  • Anion gap >12 is consistent with DKA but not required for diagnosis.
  • Intubation should be avoided whenever possible; if needed, use rocuronium for RSI (succinylcholine may exacerbate electrolyte imbalance).
  • Potassium: if <3.5 mEq/L, give potassium until ≥3.5 before starting insulin.
  • If K 3.5–5.0 mEq/L, start insulin and IV potassium supplementation; if >5.0, start insulin alone.
  • Patients with ESRD do not develop osmotic diuresis; reserve potassium supplementation for hypokalemia <3.5 mEq/L.
  • Cerebral edema is a leading cause of death in children with DKA but is very rare in adults.
  • Most common triggers: infection or non-adherence to insulin therapy.

Attending Pearls

  • Fluids come first; worry about fluid type later, but get volume in early.
  • These patients are total-body potassium depleted even if serum levels are normal or slightly elevated.
  • Save sodium bicarbonate for dire situations like severe hyperkalemia, arrest, or shock from acidosis.
  • Avoid intubation whenever possible; it can make acidosis profoundly worse and cause arrest.
  • Cerebral edema diagnosis is clinical, not based on imaging.

Pitfalls

  • Intubating a DKA patient can cause cardiac arrest due to worsening acidosis.
  • Giving insulin before checking potassium can cause fatal hypokalemia.
  • Routinely giving sodium bicarbonate for acidosis is not helpful and may precipitate hypokalemia.
  • Forgetting to turn off an insulin pump before starting IV insulin.
  • Giving aggressive fluids/potassium in ESRD patients without shock or hypokalemia.
