Altered Mental Status: Approach and Differential

Neurology · Week 19 · src: Approach to Altered Mental Status
Bottom line: Altered mental status: check glucose within 5 min, treat hypoglycemia <55 mg/dL, and get core temp early.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Place on cardiac monitor, continuous pulse oximetry; secure IV access; obtain point-of-care glucose.
↓
2Use PPE and consider communicable diseases (eg, COVID-19) in any altered patient.
↓
3Maintain C-spine precautions; assume trauma unless history clearly excludes it; keep advanced airway equipment at bedside.
↓
4For RSI when hyperkalemia likely (ESRD, heat stroke, rhabdomyolysis), use a non-depolarizing paralytic such as rocuronium.
↓
5Reverse quickly reversible causes before intubation: hypoglycemia (D50 12.5-50 g IV; glucagon 1 mg IM if no IV) and opioid overdose (naloxone).
↓
6Provide oxygen by nasal cannula, non-rebreather, or NIPPV; maintain SpO2 >90%; obtain ABG/VBG if hypercarbia considered.
↓
7Give IV fluids for hypotension/hypoperfusion; obtain ECG; follow ACLS for hemodynamically significant dysrhythmia.
↓
8Check focal neuro findings and pupils; treat glucose <55 mg/dL; consider non-contrast head CT.
↓
9If non-convulsive status epilepticus suspected, obtain EEG if possible, consult Neurology immediately, consider benzodiazepines then phenytoin/fosphenytoin, valproic acid, or levetiracetam.
↓
10Assess for trauma, drug use, pain-medication patches, infection; review environmental and medication history; check core temperature.
↓
11Treat hypothermia <35°C with forced air convection (Bair Hugger) and warm IV fluids; more aggressive measures if cardiovascularly unstable.
↓
12Treat hyperthermia >40°C with evaporative cooling, cold water on skin, fan, ice packs to axilla/groin/neck/torso, or cold saline bladder irrigation.

⏱ Time Targets

Point-of-care glucose in altered mental statuswithin first 5 minutesCommon, reversible, easily tested cause; check in field and repeat on arrival.
Core temperature measurementwithin first few minutes of resuscitationDelays of half an hour are not acceptable; must be a real (rectal) core temperature, not temporal.
Empiric antibiotics for sepsis/meningitiswithin first hourMost recent core measures; do not delay for CT.

📝 Key Facts

  • Altered mental status = impairment of level of consciousness (alertness, attention), cognition (orientation, memory, executive function), and/or behavior; exists along a spectrum; coma is the most severe state (unresponsive to verbal or painful stimuli).
  • Treat hypoglycemia if glucose <55 mg/dL (<3.1 mmol/L) with dextrose 50% in water 12.5-50 g IV push, repeat if needed.
  • AEIOU TIPS: A alcohol/acidosis; E endocrine, epilepsy, electrolyte, encephalopathy; I infection; O opiates/overdose; U uremia; T trauma; I insulin/ischemia; P poisoning/psychosis; S stroke, seizure, syncope.
  • Core temperature cutoffs: hypothermia <35°C; hyperthermia >40°C.
  • Naloxone profile: unconscious, pinpoint pupils, breathing slowly or not at all; higher doses (eg, 10 mg) may be required with atypical opioids including fentanyl and methadone.
  • Opioid overdose in cardiac arrest: naloxone 2 mg; in slow breathing with needle track or pills: naloxone 0.4 mg in increments to avoid the patient jumping up and punching you.
  • One amp D50 = 50 mL of 50% glucose = only 25 g of sugar (about half a Snickers bar); profoundly hypoglycemic patients may need two amps up front.
  • Pediatric hypoglycemia: 2.5 mL/kg of 10% glucose solution; glucagon releases glucose from liver and works poorly in chronic alcoholics, malnourished, or anorexic patients with no liver stores.
  • Thiamine 100 mg IV upfront in any patient who is alcoholic or could be thiamine deficient (also malnutrition, chronic vomiting, cancer), along with glucose.
  • For suspected meningitis: dexamethasone 10 mg with or before broad-spectrum antibiotics with good CNS penetration, eg, ceftriaxone 2 g IV; if altered or seizure beforehand, many add acyclovir for possible herpes encephalitis.
  • Zinc: not in this topic.
  • Hyperglycemic emergencies: DKA (type 1) and hyperglycemic hyperosmotic syndrome (type 2) are a continuum; initial treatment is the same — IV fluids for dehydration.
  • Immunocompromised patients (HIV/AIDS, elderly, cancer chemotherapy) may not mount a fever; a low temp (95°F, 94°F) can also tip you off to sepsis.
  • GCS range: 14 = talking but totally confused; 3-8 = comatose, not talking, not opening eyes.
  • Uremic encephalopathy: consider in ESRD/severe renal insufficiency; tip-offs include missed dialysis, AV fistula or dialysis catheter, asterixis; the most emergent issue is hyperkalemia on ECG (eg, K 7) — start hyperkalemia protocol and dialysis.
  • Stroke CT: acute ischemic stroke CT is usually normal for many, many hours; hemorrhagic strokes (subarachnoid, hypertensive hemorrhage) are visible on CT and prompt immediate neurosurgical involvement and clotting considerations.
  • If temporal thermometer is used, it is not a core temperature — oxygen is blasting into it and the patient is not closing their mouth; this drives me nuts.
  • Toxicologic physical exam: pupils and reactivity, skin (dry vs wet), bowel sounds (hyperactive vs hypoactive), bladder (full) help identify the toxin class.
  • Psychosis should be a last-resort, diagnosis-of-exclusion in the ED; it is the emergency provider's responsibility to rule out the badness, and let someone else make that diagnosis as an inpatient after full workup including MRI.

Attending Pearls

  • Glucose is basically a vital sign; check it early, and recheck it because it can go down again even 10-15 minutes later.
  • LP is never an emergency; if the patient is circling the drain, wait until they stabilize — you are already giving antibiotics and getting a CT.
  • Trauma and medical are not exclusive: maybe this is someone who had a stroke and then fell down and broke their hip — everyone worries about the hip and not why they fell.
  • If somebody is profoundly hypoglycemic and you give them a half a Snickers bar (25 g), you will probably have to give them the other half.
  • In the emergency setting, do not make a diagnosis of psychosis — your job is to go through the algorithm and make sure nothing else bad is there; let someone else make that diagnosis as an inpatient after full workup including MRI.

Pitfalls

  • Assuming the patient is 'just drunk' — chronic alcoholics are at extraordinarily high risk for subdural, intracranial bleeds of all types, meningitis, and other infections; even if intoxicated, they may be hiding something else.
  • Doing the temperature too late (sometimes half an hour is not acceptable) or using a temporal thermometer and assuming it is core; it is not a core temperature.
  • Delaying or skipping glucose check — if you are not checking glucose right up front within the first few minutes, you are making a huge mistake.
  • Giving thiamine after glucose in the alcoholic/thiamine-deficient patient (traditional teaching: give thiamine before or with glucose to avoid further brain damage).
  • Labeling an altered patient psychotic without a full medical workup — missed encephalitis cases get put on psychiatric hold.
