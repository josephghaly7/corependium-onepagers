Isolated Head Trauma

Trauma · Week 16 · src: Pediatric Head Trauma major and minor
Bottom line: In head trauma, prevent hypoxemia and hypotension; keep SpO2 92-98%, MAP >=80, CPP 55-60.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Maintain SpO2 >92% and <98%; intubate and ventilate if GCS <=8.
↓
2Target PaCO2 35-45 mm Hg and end-tidal CO2 30-40 mm Hg; correlate capnography with blood gas early.
↓
3Do not perform prophylactic hyperventilation.
↓
4Monitor BP and immediately treat hypotension; reverse low BP with norepinephrine or push-dose epinephrine.
↓
5Target MAP at least 80 (assume ICP ~20 gives CPP 55-60).
↓
6Give hypertonic saline: 3% NaCl 5 mL/kg IV over 15 min, or sodium bicarbonate 1-2 amps over ~5 min per amp.
↓
7Give mannitol 0.25-1 g/kg IV over 20-30 min if used; match urine output with normal saline to avoid hypotension.
↓
8For elevated ICP, keep head of bed at 30° and maintain normal glucose and normothermia.
↓
9Start seizure prophylaxis with levetiracetam for moderate to severe TBI or traumatic ICH; treat active seizures as status epilepticus instead.
↓
10For post-intubation sedation, prefer propofol infusion 20-30 mcg/kg/min; small 20 mg boluses if needed.
↓
11If propofol drip started without bolus, push 2-3 mL to clear IV dead space.
↓
12Obtain emergency non-contrast head CT and neurosurgical consultation for altered mental status, focal deficits, or penetrating trauma.

📝 Key Facts

  • Endotracheal intubation and controlled ventilation for GCS <=8.
  • Oxygen saturation >92% and <98%; PaCO2 35-45 mm Hg; end-tidal CO2 30-40 mm Hg.
  • Hypotension lower limits: SBP <60 mm Hg in term neonates (0-28 days); <70 mm Hg in infants (1-12 months); <70 + (2 x age) mm Hg in children 1-10 years; <90 mm Hg in children >=10 years.
  • Admission SBP below the 75th percentile after severe TBI is associated with elevated in-hospital mortality.
  • Children <4 years old and those with hypotension, low GCS, coagulopathy, or hyperglycemia have the highest morbidity and mortality.
  • Minor head injury: normal mental status, no abnormal neurologic deficits, and no physical evidence of skull fracture.
  • Isolated head trauma should cause hypertension; hypotension suggests missed bleeding, herniation, medication, neurogenic shock, or stress cardiomyopathy.
  • Seizure prophylaxis applies to traumatic ICH, not spontaneous or aneurysmal bleeds.
  • Propofol decreases cerebral metabolic rate, oxygen requirements, and ICP; it is eminently titratable.

Attending Pearls

  • Hypotension and malperfusion, especially in the first 24 hours, are incredibly deleterious.
  • Episodes of hypotension are killers in severe brain injury.
  • If propofol vasodilation is the problem, eliminate it with a vasoconstrictor.
  • Exam preservation is less important than using sedatives that decrease cerebral metabolic rate and ICP.
  • Start low and go slow.

Pitfalls

  • Leaving the hypotensive patient while waiting 15-20 minutes for a liter of saline.
  • Giving mannitol without matching urine output with normal saline, causing hypotension.
  • Using large propofol boluses that cause wide blood pressure swings.
  • Starting a propofol drip without clearing IV dead space.
  • Performing prophylactic hyperventilation.
