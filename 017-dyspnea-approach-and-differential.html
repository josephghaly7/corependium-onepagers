📝 Key Facts

  • Acute dyspnea accounts for nearly 4 million ED visits annually in the US.
  • In patients >65 years, dyspnea is one of the most common presenting complaints.
  • GCS <8 is an indication to secure the airway.
  • Dyspnea is subjective; 90% of cases are cardiopulmonary, but other systems can be involved.
  • Oxygen therapy: excess O2 may be harmful in MI, pneumonia, ICU patients; avoid routine high-flow.
  • COPD patients may live with sats in the 80s; titrate O2 carefully.
  • Methemoglobinemia: ~1.5 g of affected hemoglobin can cause clinical cyanosis.
  • Carbon monoxide poisoning: can cause headache, flu-like symptoms, worse in morning; consider if whole family including dog affected.

Attending Pearls

  • Dyspnea is one of the coolest chief complaints because it speaks to a sensation people can't put into words.
  • You have to think airway first, even though this isn't a patient you're intubating this second.
  • For every patient presenting with acute dyspnea, treat as if they could become critical quickly.
  • BiPAP doesn't discriminate; it can help CHF, COPD, asthma, and even pneumonia.
  • Psychiatric causes of dyspnea are a diagnosis of exclusion; always assume organic first.

Pitfalls

  • Anchoring on cardiopulmonary causes and missing airway, neurologic, hematologic, toxic, or metabolic etiologies.
  • Giving 100% oxygen indiscriminately; excess O2 may be harmful.
  • Using BiPAP in patients with severely altered mental status (relative contraindication; risk of aspiration).
  • Attributing dyspnea to psychiatric causes without excluding organic causes, especially PE in young patients.
  • Missing neuromuscular weakness: patients may look well but can rapidly fatigue and require intubation.
