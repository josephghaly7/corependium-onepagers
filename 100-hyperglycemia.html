Hyperglycemia

Endocrine · Week 44 · src: Hyperglycemia
Bottom line: Hyperglycemia >600 mg/dL needs labs, ECG, IV fluids, insulin; discharge number not evidence-based.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Place on cardiac monitor and continuous pulse oximetry; secure IV access; obtain point-of-care glucose.
↓
2If altered mental status, vital sign instability, or shock, consider DKA/HHS, sepsis, ischemia (MI, CVA, mesenteric, limb).
↓
3Intubate if unable to protect airway; preoxygenate; use non-depolarizing agent like rocuronium for RSI.
↓
4Give 1-2 L isotonic crystalloid IV (pediatrics: 10-20 mL/kg); obtain ECG for MI or electrolyte abnormalities.
↓
5For moderate hyperglycemia (200-600 mg/dL), consider renal function/electrolytes; consider IV fluids and insulin.
↓
6For severe hyperglycemia (>600 mg/dL), obtain BMP, CBC, venous blood gas, UA, ECG; give IV fluids and insulin.
↓
7In non-critically-ill patients, if asymptomatic with clear cause (e.g., missed meds), may do no labs and follow up.
↓
8If ruling out DKA, get chemistry panel, urine dip, and serum beta-hydroxybutyrate if available.
↓
9Give 1-2 L IV fluids; oral rehydration acceptable if awake, alert, gut working; recheck glucose; insulin generally not needed.
↓
10Discharge uncomplicated hyperglycemia when glucose readable on glucometer; no threshold proven to affect short-term outcomes.

💊 Doses

Normal saline1-2 L IVInitial hydration; Plasma-Lyte or lactated Ringer's are alternatives.
Isotonic crystalloid (pediatric)10-20 mL/kg IVFluid resuscitation in pediatric patients.

📝 Key Facts

  • Moderate hyperglycemia: 200-600 mg/dL (11.1-33.3 mmol/L); severe: >600 mg/dL (>33.3 mmol/L).
  • 1-2 L isotonic crystalloid IV for adults; 10-20 mL/kg for pediatric patients.
  • Diabetes is immune-suppressing and targets every organ system; check skin and all orifices.
  • Urine ketones measure acetoacetate, not beta-hydroxybutyrate, which is 10 times more common in DKA.
  • Serum beta-hydroxybutyrate is more sensitive and specific for DKA but not widely available.
  • 350 mg/dL cutoff studies found no increase in ED bounce-backs or hospitalizations with higher discharge glucose.
  • 600 mg/dL equals about 33 mmol/L in SI units.
  • Glucometers reading 'hi' mean the true value is unknown and could be as high as 1800 mg/dL.

Attending Pearls

  • Fluids alone bring down glucose in most asymptomatic patients; insulin is usually unnecessary.
  • There is no evidence-based magic discharge number; 350 and 500 are arbitrary clinician thresholds.
  • Oral rehydration is fine if the patient is awake, alert, and has a functioning gut.
  • Education is critical: patients may hydrate with sugary soda, worsening hyperglycemia.
  • For suspected DKA, use non-depolarizing neuromuscular blockade (e.g., rocuronium) for RSI to avoid exacerbating electrolyte abnormalities.

Pitfalls

  • Treating the number without hunting for the trigger: infection, ischemia, missed meds, or new organ dysfunction.
  • Relying on urine ketones to rule out DKA because they miss beta-hydroxybutyrate.
  • Giving insulin when fluids alone will lower glucose; sugar rebounds once oral meds are resumed.
  • Starting metformin without a follow-up plan, leaving the patient with no one to manage side effects.
  • Using a non-depolarizing agent like rocuronium? (Actually rule says use non-depolarizing, so pitfall is using depolarizing succinylcholine.
