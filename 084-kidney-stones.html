Kidney Stones

Renal and Urogenital · Week 40 · src: Renal Stone Disease
Bottom line: Renal colic: accurate diagnosis, pain control, obstruction assessment; 90% of stones pass spontaneously.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Take history and exam; consider stone risk and red flags for vascular causes.
↓
2If older, hypertensive, or atypical pain, evaluate for AAA/dissection before assuming stone.
↓
3Obtain urinalysis; absence of hematuria does not rule out stone (15% have none).
↓
4Choose imaging selectively; ultrasound first to avoid radiation; CT if toxic, febrile, or high-risk features.
↓
5Assess for hydronephrosis, but interpret with clinical context.
↓
6Provide analgesia; NSAIDs first-line, add opioids if severe or refractory.
↓
7Estimate stone size and location; 90% pass spontaneously, average 1 week but up to 1 month.
↓
8Admit if obstructed and infected, intractable pain/vomiting, AKI, single kidney, or failed outpatient therapy (4-6 weeks).
↓
9For infected obstructed stone, consult urology/IR for urgent decompression.
↓
10Discharge most with analgesia, antiemetics, antibiotics if indicated, and urology follow-up.

📝 Key Facts

  • Lifetime kidney stone risk 10-13% men, 7% women; peak age 40-49.
  • Hematuria present in 85-90% of cases; 33% have <5 RBC/HPF, 19% <1 RBC/HPF, 11% no RBCs.
  • CT identifies alternative diagnoses in ~10.8% of renal colic cases.
  • Hydronephrosis absent in 11-15% of urolithiasis; if present, obstructing stone likely >5 mm.
  • STONE score predictors: male sex, <6 hours pain, non-Black, vomiting, hematuria.
  • Urine culture positive in 36.4% with pyuria (>10 WBC/HPF) or positive leukocyte esterase.
  • Recurrence rate 15% at 1 year, 30-50% at 10 years.
  • Stone composition: calcium oxalate/phosphate 75%, struvite 15%, uric acid 10%, cystine 1%.

Attending Pearls

  • Acute ED management focuses on three things: accurate diagnosis, pain control, and assessing obstruction—not stone composition.
  • If you cannot control pain, think you are missing something else, especially a vascular emergency.
  • In older patients with hypertension, consider AAA before settling on renal colic.
  • Stone size matters: larger stones are less likely to pass without urologic intervention.
  • A stone in the kidney that is not obstructing is usually painless; pain starts when it leaves the ureteropelvic junction.

Pitfalls

  • Ruling out stone because urinalysis shows no blood; 15% of stone patients have no hematuria.
  • Sending every patient for CT; ultrasound can be used first in many to avoid radiation.
  • Using hydronephrosis alone to decide admission or discharge; pain control and clinical context matter.
  • Assuming stones pass in a few days; average 1 week but can take up to 1 month or require intervention.
  • Missing AAA/dissection in older patients with flank pain; it is the number one fatal misdiagnosis.
