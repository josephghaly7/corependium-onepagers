Necrotizing Soft Tissue Infections Part 2

Dermatology · Week 49 · src: Skin and Soft Tissue Infections
Bottom line: NSTI: early broad antibiotics, resuscitation, and prompt OR source control.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1If toxic with clinical NSTI evidence, bypass imaging and go directly to OR for source control.
↓
2Start resuscitation early: IV access, crystalloid, electrolytes, glucose control before OR.
↓
3If septic, give IV fluids and consider early vasopressors if not responding.
↓
4Send cultures and start broad-spectrum antibiotics ASAP; do not delay OR.
↓
5Cover MRSA, gram-negatives, anaerobes; use piperacillin-tazobactam, clindamycin, vancomycin or carbapenem.
↓
6If severe beta-lactam allergy, use aminoglycoside or fluoroquinolone plus metronidazole.
↓
7If observing cellulitis, watch carefully; worsening pain, tachycardia, or fever means OR.
↓
8If no imaging, perform finger test: local anesthesia, 2 cm incision to deep fascia.
↓
9Probe along deep fascia; little resistance or dishwater fluid confirms necrotizing fasciitis.
↓
10In OR, make counter incisions if initial incision looks normal; infection may hide.

⏱ Time Targets

OR delay0 minNothing should delay OR once NSTI diagnosed; fascial necrosis 2-3 cm/hr.
Second look surgery~24 hrTypically go back for second look about 24 hours later.

💊 Doses

Clindamycin600 mg IV every 8 hoursHigher concentration for necrotizing fasciitis; inhibits toxin production.
Piperacillin-tazobactamCombined with clindamycin and vancomycin as go-to regimen.
VancomycinFor MRSA coverage.
LinezolidAlternative to vancomycin for MRSA.
CarbapenemCan substitute for piperacillin-tazobactam.
Aminoglycoside or fluoroquinolone plus metronidazoleFor severe beta-lactam allergy.

📝 Key Facts

  • Fascial necrosis can advance 2-3 cm per hour; nothing should delay OR.
  • Pain out of proportion raises suspicion; painless NSTI can occur.
  • Erythema and swelling are most common; bullae, skin necrosis, crepitus are late.
  • LRINEC score, CT, and ultrasound cannot rule out NSTI.
  • Clindamycin 600 mg every 8 hours inhibits toxin production and kills non-replicating bacteria.
  • Eagle effect is paradoxical beta-lactam inactivity at high bacterial concentrations.
  • Finger test: 2 cm incision to deep fascia; little resistance or dishwater fluid confirms.
  • Insufficient evidence for routine IVIG or hyperbarics; never delay surgery for them.

Attending Pearls

  • Source control is key; everything else stabilizes until OR.
  • Start antibiotics early; earlier targeted therapy improves outcomes.
  • NSTI can occur in healthy patients after minor injury.
  • If observing cellulitis, worsening is a definitive indicator for OR.
  • Finger test: if tissues move apart with little resistance, you have your diagnosis.

Pitfalls

  • Relying on LRINEC or imaging to rule out NSTI.
  • Delaying OR for antibiotics, hyperbarics, or IVIG.
  • Assuming unimpressive external appearance or normal initial incision excludes NSTI.
  • Failing to start resuscitation before transport to OR.
  • Starting metronidazole first; unlikely dying of anaerobic sepsis.
