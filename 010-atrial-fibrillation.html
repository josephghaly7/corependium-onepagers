Atrial Fibrillation

Cardiology · Week 5 · src: Atrial Fibrillation
Bottom line: AF with RVR: rate or rhythm control; stroke risk is one-sixth of CVAs.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1Obtain 12-lead ECG, place on cardiac monitor, and get IV access.
↓
2Treat underlying etiology (sepsis, volume depletion, PE); AF with RVR often not the cause of decompensation.
↓
3Consider synchronized cardioversion at 200 J biphasic if shock, active ischemia, frank pulmonary edema, or decreased mental status.
↓
4For pre-excitation with AF and RVR, use synchronized cardioversion at 200 J biphasic as preferred treatment.
↓
5Avoid rate control if hypotensive, low LV filling pressures, or low SVR (sepsis).
↓
6Consider restoring sinus rhythm when low LV contractility contributes to shock.
↓
7For IV rate control, give diltiazem 0.25 mg/kg IV bolus over 2 minutes.
↓
8If rate not controlled, give diltiazem 0.35 mg/kg IV push 15 minutes after initial bolus.
↓
9If boluses fail, start diltiazem infusion at 5 mg/hour, titrate by 5 mg/hour every 15 minutes (max 15 mg/hour).
↓
10Consider lower diltiazem dose in older adults (e.g., 10 mg IV loading dose).

📝 Key Facts

  • AF is the most common dysrhythmia in the United States.
  • Atrial flutter sawtooth pattern rate is 240-360 bpm unless affected by medication.
  • AF is responsible for one-sixth of all cerebrovascular accidents (CVAs).
  • AF patients have double the mortality of patients in sinus rhythm.
  • Synchronized cardioversion for AF with RVR in pre-excitation uses 200 J biphasic.
  • Hold rate control if SBP <90 mmHg or heart rate <60 bpm.
  • Metoprolol maximum total dose is 15 mg IV.
  • Diltiazem maximum infusion rate is 15 mg/hour.

Attending Pearls

  • AF with rapid ventricular response is rarely immediately life-threatening but may result from other life-threatening pathophysiologies.
  • Future risk of stroke is the major concern, as AF causes one-sixth of all CVAs.
  • The shock doesn't cause embolism; a pre-existing clot embolizes once the atrium contracts again.
  • Nodal blockers control rate but do not convert rhythm and carry no conversion stroke risk.
  • Most patients with AF or atrial flutter can be safely discharged home if close outpatient follow-up is assured.

Pitfalls

  • Assuming AF with rapid ventricular response is the cause of decompensation; evaluate for underlying etiology (sepsis, volume depletion, PE).
  • Using rate control in hypotensive patients or those with low LV filling pressures or low SVR (sepsis).
  • Assuming stroke risk only occurs with electrical cardioversion; drug and spontaneous conversion carry equal risk.
  • Confusing rate-control nodal blockers (beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, digoxin) with rhythm-control antiarrhythmics (amiodarone, sotalol).
  • Thinking rhythm control is proven superior; no good evidence shows better long-term outcomes versus rate control.
