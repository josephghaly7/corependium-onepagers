Infant Fever 2 to 3 Months

Pediatrics · Week 25 · src: CorePendium Pediatric Fever and Serious Bacterial Infections, Pediatric Soft Tissue Infections
Bottom line: Febrile 2-3 month olds: if ill-appearing full workup; well-appearing get UA, +UA gets blood culture and inflammatory markers.

⚖ Management Pathway

● assess/decide   ● act/treat   ● refer/transfer
1For 60-90 day febrile infants, determine ill-appearing versus well-appearing.
↓
2If ill-appearing, perform full febrile workup and give antibiotics.
↓
3If well-appearing, obtain urinalysis; non-catheter screening acceptable.
↓
4If urinalysis negative, use shared decision-making; may stop workup without additional labs.
↓
5If urinalysis positive, obtain blood culture and inflammatory markers (CBC, CRP, procalcitonin).
↓
6If fever >39°C in >60-day-old, consider inflammatory markers; if elevated, blood culture and empiric ceftriaxone.
↓
7For ex-premature infants, avoid stopping workup; standard pathways not validated.
↓
8If UTI in >21-day-old with normal inflammatory markers, no lumbar puncture needed.
↓
9If infant <21 days with fever or UTI, perform robust workup including lumbar puncture.
↓
10Viral testing: >28 days, fever <38.5, positive flu/RSV may stop; <28 days, no change.

💊 Doses

Ceftriaxone50 mg/kg IV every 12 hours (max 2,000 mg)Suspected sepsis/meningitis; part of regimen 1
Ceftazidime50-70 mg/kg IV every 8 hours (max single dose 2,000 mg)Suspected sepsis/meningitis; part of regimen 2
Vancomycin1 month to 8 years: 20 mg/kg IV every 6 hours (max 900 mg); 9-16 years: 17.5 mg/kg IV every 6 hours (max 900 mg)Suspected sepsis/meningitis; part of regimen 1 and 2
Dexamethasone0.15 mg/kg IV every 6 hours (max single dose 10 mg)Suspected bacterial meningitis; administer before antibiotics when possible

📝 Key Facts

  • Well-appearing 60-90 day infants with negative urinalysis had ~0.5% bacteremia and no meningitis.
  • Ill-appearing infants >60 days have high rates of invasive bacterial infections and need full workup and antibiotics.
  • Ex-premature infants were excluded from PCARN and step-by-step; risk decreases until ~48 weeks postmenstrual age.
  • AAP: >21 days with UTI and negative inflammatory markers do not necessarily need LP; >4,000 LPs to catch one meningitis.
  • Positive viral panel should not change decisions in infants <28 days; in >28 days bacteremia lower but not zero.
  • At authors' institution, >28 days with fever <38.5 and positive influenza or RSV, workup stopped.
  • Fever in infant is >38°C or 100.4°F; rectal temperature is most accurate.
  • Premature infants more likely to have invasive bacterial infections and present at later chronologic age than term controls.

Attending Pearls

  • Bladder percussion with lumbosacral massage can yield clean urine; may avoid catheterization, especially in males.
  • Mark anterior and posterior spinal canal depths on LP needle using ultrasound and manometer to prevent bloody taps.
  • Remove stylet as soon as through skin in infant LP because spinal canal is superficial.
  • Keep non-dominant index finger on iliac crest and thumbnail on L4-L5 interspace throughout infant LP.
  • Knowing what to do with that febrile infant isn't always easy. Good luck out there.

Pitfalls

  • Applying PCARN/step-by-step pathways to ex-premature infants is not validated.
  • Using positive viral panel to rule out bacterial infection in infants <28 days.
  • Performing LP in >21-day-old with UTI and normal inflammatory markers.
  • Back-walling during infant LP causes bloody tap; mark ultrasound depths on needle.
